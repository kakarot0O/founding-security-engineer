# Review findings: reference library
Three independent review passes over the 25 authored files, 2026-08-25. 81 findings: 15 blocker, 40 major, 26 minor. Kept verbatim so nothing is lost between patch passes. Applied findings are struck through with the commit or date.

## Pass 1: Completeness (seasoned head of security)
**Reviewer verdict:** This is unusually strong material: the 2019 grid is faithfully sourced and honestly delta'd, the five modern cells are the right five, and the operational texture (danger zones, failure modes with early tells, copy-pasteable messages, honest cost bands, the never-mark-done-without-evidence discipline) is better than most published first-security-hire guidance. The gaps cluster at the edges the original grid never had rather than inside the cells it does cover. Four are severe: there is no resilience story at all (no backups, restore drill, or destructive-event runbook anywhere in 10,300 lines, while CO-1 and CO-2 tell the hire to publish claims about exactly that), no way to answer "are we already compromised" on day one before short log retention closes the window, no product-security content for the two most likely customer-data breach paths (tenant isolation for B2B, account takeover for B2C, despite B2C being named the single most important branching question and then never serviced), and no path for a company that is not cooperative or not standard (hostile or decorative mandate with no personal-liability guidance, agency-built with no internal engineers, regulated, post-Series-B, or inheriting a predecessor's program). Separately, the state-file specification contradicts itself on modern-cell identifiers, context-stack format, and frame ceilings, which matters more than usual because the design depends on the agent parsing its own prior output across sessions.

### Blocker (7)

**B1.1 `ALL`**

*Problem.* There is no backup, restore, or destructive-event coverage anywhere in the 25 files. "Backup" appears only as passing mentions: a questionnaire answer template (CO-2 Q16), a column in the CO-4 data inventory, and a DR-1 danger-zone line about restoring over live data. There is no discovery, no walk, no restore drill, no immutability guidance, and no runbook for the case the set's own threat model implies: an attacker who has stolen a session and now holds the rights to delete the cloud account, the database, or the SaaS tenant. Meanwhile CO-1 section 7 tells the hire to publish backup and restore-testing claims and CO-2 Q16 tells them to state RTO and RPO, using facts the set never taught them how to obtain. Ransomware appears five times, all in passing lists.

*Fix.* Add `references/m-6-backups-and-recovery.md` as a sixth modern cell. Discovery per provider (RDS/Aurora automated backups and PITR window, S3 versioning plus Object Lock, GCP and Azure equivalents, plus SaaS-native export for the code host, CRM, and document platform). Three questions: what would we lose, how long to restore, can the production identity delete the backups. Then the controls: a cross-account or cross-project immutable copy, and a timed restore drill whose recorded time-to-restore becomes the evidence for CO-2 Q16 and CO-1 section 7 so the claim is never published without proof. Add a destructive-event runbook that plugs into DR-1 (containment when the attacker holds the deletion rights). Add M-6 rows to the SECURITY-STATE template and a Gate C step in 03-90-day-plan.md.

**B1.2 `references/00-cold-start.md`**

*Problem.* Day one assumes a clean environment. Cold start Phase 6 hunts for misconfigurations, 01-recon builds an inventory, and DR-2 builds detections for future events. Nothing answers the question the hire is actually there to answer when they were hired after a scare: is someone in here right now, or were they last quarter? This is time-limited evidence. DR-3 states that default retention is commonly 7 to 90 days, so the log window closes while the hire is drawing architecture diagrams. The single highest-yield check for the set's own stated dominant threat chain (infostealer to session to SaaS to cloud), a tenant-wide sweep for mail forwarding and inbox rules, appears exactly once in the entire file set, buried in the CS-3 leaver checklist.

*Fix.* Add `references/dr-0-compromise-assessment.md`, run unconditionally in week one and loaded from cold start Phase 4 whenever the answer to intake question 7 ("why now?") involves any incident or scare. Read-only hunt list with console path per platform: tenant-wide mail forwarding and inbox rules; OAuth grants added in the last 12 months carrying mail, drive, or repo scopes; MFA methods registered or reset; admin role grants; new IAM users and CreateAccessKey events; new SSH keys, deploy keys, personal access tokens, and self-hosted runners on the code host; admin sign-ins from unexpected geographies. Each entry states what a hit means, with a hard rule that any hit stops recon and opens DR-1. First instruction in the file: record the retention window of every source before doing anything else, because you may have days of evidence left.

**B1.3 `references/03-90-day-plan.md`**

*Problem.* The "company had a breach recently" adaptation treats a prior incident purely as political capital and a source of pre-agreed remediation items. It never considers the far more dangerous and equally common version: the prior incident was never properly scoped, never notified, or notified late, and the new hire is now the only person who knows. There is no guidance on discovering an unmet notification obligation for a past event, which is a live legal exposure for the company and a personal one for the hire from the moment they write a conclusion into a durable file. DR-1's notification decision tree is written entirely for an incident that starts today.

*Fix.* Add a section to dr-1-incident-response-plan.md titled "An incident that happened before you arrived". Rules: reconstruct the timeline from artifacts before asking anyone anything; do not write a conclusion about whether a breach occurred or whether notification was required into any durable file; route to counsel first and ask whether the review should run under privilege, reusing the pattern co-3 already establishes for the commitment register; the decision to notify late belongs to the CEO and counsel and never to the security hire; and if the company declines to act on advice given in writing, that is a RISK-REGISTER.md acceptance with a named accepter, which is the record that protects the hire.

**B1.4 `ALL`**

*Problem.* Business-to-consumer is named as the single most important branch and then never serviced. Cold start's first question is B2B versus B2C, described as "the question that changes everything downstream", and the stated answer for B2C is that "account takeover, abuse, and privacy dominate". No cell covers any of it. "trust and safety" appears zero times, "credential stuffing" three times in passing, and account takeover only as an identity-provider detection for employees. A B2C hire following the default sequence spends 90 days on corporate identity and a questionnaire knowledge base for questionnaires that will never arrive, while mass compromise of end-user accounts, the thing that actually triggers their notification obligation, is untouched.

*Fix.* Add `references/se-5-consumer-account-security.md`: end-user auth review (password reset and email-change flows, session invalidation on password change, MFA availability and recovery-code handling), zero-cost credential-stuffing defenses (breached-password check at signup and login, per-account and per-IP rate limits, user-visible device and session listing), abuse and fraud signals, the account-recovery social engineering path through support, and the operational duty at consumer scale (bulk-ATO detection and mass-notification readiness). Add a B2C branch to "Adapting the plan" in 03-90-day-plan.md that replaces CO-1 and CO-2 in Gate D with this cell, and swap the questionnaire metrics in 05-metrics-and-comms.md for ATO rate and support-driven account recovery volume.

**B1.5 `references/se-2-understand-the-tech-stack.md`**

*Problem.* For a multi-tenant B2B SaaS the likeliest route to a customer-data breach is a broken authorization check, not a misconfigured bucket, and the set has no discovery and no test for it. "tenant isolation" appears once, "cross-tenant" once, "IDOR" zero times. SE-1's review template asks where the authorization check lives, but nothing tells the hire how to actually verify it. The set correctly forbids scanning production and then offers no sanctioned alternative, so a beginner has no path at all to the question every enterprise buyer and every real attacker asks first.

*Fix.* Add a "Tenant isolation and object-level authorization" section to se-2: where the tenant identifier enters the request, whether the data layer scopes by it centrally or per query, and a grep pass for queries fetching by primary key with no tenant predicate. Add an authorized test procedure to se-1: create two test tenants you own, on staging, with written authorization under the existing rules of engagement, and attempt cross-tenant read on each object type and each API. Make "show me a query that fetches by identifier without a tenant scope" a standing design-review question in the SE-1 template.

**B1.6 `references/05-metrics-and-comms.md`**

*Problem.* There is no path for a hostile or decorative mandate, and no personal-liability guidance. Part 7 covers four political failure modes and Part 4 covers handling a "no", but all of it assumes good faith. The set repeatedly instructs the hire to get a founder to co-sign, endorse, announce, and pre-authorise. It never addresses the mandate being decorative (hired to satisfy an investor or answer one questionnaire), access never being granted, a founder overriding controls and expecting silence, or the hire being positioned to absorb blame. Critically, nothing tells the hire what to do when asked to sign, say, or publish something they believe is false. CO-1 and CO-2 correctly call inaccurate claims a misrepresentation risk to the company, but the hire's own exposure is never named and no procedure is given. This is the highest-consequence thing a beginner does not know.

*Fix.* Add `references/08-when-it-is-not-working.md` with three parts. (a) Diagnosis at day 30 and day 60: named signals such as no access after two escalations of the 02-intake ladder, no decision on any critical row in RISK-REGISTER.md, executive exemption from every control, and exclusion from incidents, each with a fixable-or-not test. (b) The refusal procedure: never sign, send, or publish a security claim you cannot evidence; state the disagreement in writing once, factually; require whoever wants it to own it in DECISION-LOG.md; if that is refused, the refusal is itself the finding and goes to the board or audit committee. (c) The exit case: what to preserve (your own dated record, never company data), how to leave without burning the record, and an explicit statement that leaving is a legitimate outcome. Add a routing line for it in SKILL.md.

**B1.7 `ALL`**

*Problem.* A company with no engineering team, only contractors or a development agency, is unhandled. Every cell assumes internal engineers, named human owners who persist, and that the company controls its own cloud, repositories, and pipeline. A very common seed-stage reality is that an agency holds the cloud root account, owns the code host organisation, and is the only party with production access. The set's ownership rule ("a named human, not a team") is unusable when that human bills hourly and rotates off in six weeks. Nothing covers obtaining access when the vendor owns the environment, the contractual levers available, or transition of control.

*Fix.* Add `references/09-outsourced-engineering.md`. Week-one ownership map: registered owner of the domain, the cloud account or organisation, the code host organisation, the app store accounts, the DNS zone, the package registry namespace, and the payment processor, with "the agency created it under their own account" recorded as the finding rather than a footnote. Then the transition-of-control checklist to move each into a company-owned identity, the terms to put in the next SOW or MSA (named security contacts, breach notification to you, notification when their staff leave the account, no shared accounts, code and infrastructure delivered into your organisation, credential handover on termination), and the ownership substitution rule: with no internal engineer, the accountable owner is the executive who owns the vendor relationship, never the contractor. Add a matching branch to "Adapting the plan" in 03-90-day-plan.md.

### Major (12)

**M1.1 `references/co-4-data-inventory-and-framework.md`**

*Problem.* Regulated products get a decision-tree stub, not a playbook. Step 1 of the framework tree routes HIPAA and PCI to "counsel and a qualified assessor" and stops. HIPAA, PCI DSS, and FedRAMP otherwise appear almost exclusively inside "do not chase these unless a deal requires it" lists, and the 90-day adaptation for a regulated data class is three sentences. *Fix.* Add a "Regulated from day one" section to co-4 with a per-regime one-pager: the trigger test, the two or three obligations that attach immediately regardless of certification status, the required contract artifact (BAA, PCI AOC, DPA), and the single question to put to counsel. Cover HIPAA, PCI DSS, FedRAMP or StateRAMP, financial services supervision, NIS2, DORA, the EU AI Act, and SEC disclosure. In 03-90-day-plan.md, replace the three-sentence adaptation with an explicit Gate A insertion: for a regulated product the regime test and CO-3 run in week one, before the architecture diagram is finished.

**M1.2 `references/07-modern-cells.md`**

*Problem.* Vendor and third-party intake has no owner, no artifact, and no trigger. M-4 defines a sensible three-tier vendor model and the rule "no single sign-on, no customer data"; CO-4 owns the subprocessor list; CO-1 publishes it and warns that omissions may breach a DPA notification clause; CS-3 offboards non-SSO tools; DR-4 asks finance to mention new tools. Nobody owns the moment a vendor is introduced. There is no intake form, no named approver, no trigger from finance or procurement, and no defined handoff into the subprocessor list. The consequence is that CO-1's own failure mode, a customer finding a vendor you did not list, is guaranteed by the design rather than merely possible.

*Fix.* Make it one owned process. Add a `VENDOR-REGISTER.md` template to templates/README.md with M-4 tier, data categories, DPA status, SSO status, subprocessor yes or no, named owner, and review date, and state that it is the single source for the CO-1 published list and the CS-3 manual offboarding list. Add a five-question intake form to M-4 and exactly one trigger: the corporate card or accounts-payable approval step notifies security before the first charge. Name the split accountability: finance approves spend, security answers tier and data question, the requesting manager owns the vendor.

**M1.3 `ALL`**

*Problem.* Everything is calibrated to a 20-to-100 person, net-new program. Nearly every cell repeats "good enough for a 20 to 100 person startup" and then lists as "not required at this size" things that are genuinely required above it (access certification, quarterly reviews, vendor risk programs, IGA). A first hire at a 250-person Series B company inherits an existing SOC 2 with exceptions, a compliance platform on autopilot, signed tooling contracts, often an acquired company's separate identity and cloud estate, and usually a predecessor's claims already recorded as done. Nothing instructs the hire to distrust inherited state, and merger or acquisition integration appears nowhere ("M&A" and "merger" zero mentions, "acquisi" three in passing).

*Fix.* Two additions. (a) A "You are not the first, or the company is not small" section in 00-cold-start.md: treat every inherited `done` as `unknown` until re-verified with your own evidence, read the last audit's exception list and the previous hire's stated reason for leaving before anything else, and audit the compliance platform's failing controls before its passing ones. (b) An acquisition-integration branch in 03-90-day-plan.md: the acquired entity's identity provider, cloud accounts, code host, and endpoint fleet constitute a second full grid, and no tenant merge happens before the DR-0 compromise assessment has run against the acquired estate.

**M1.5 `references/cs-2-endpoint-security.md`**

*Problem.* Distributed-across-many-countries covers logistics but not the law that blocks the controls. CS-2 instructs the hire to enrol every laptop in device management and to inventory browser extensions fleet-wide; CS-4 covers shipping and travel. In several European jurisdictions a works council must be consulted before deploying endpoint monitoring, and device telemetry is employee personal data with its own lawful-basis and transparency obligations. "works council" appears exactly once, in a CS-3 danger-zone line about reading a departing employee's mail. Nothing covers the security tooling itself being a cross-border data flow (EU employee telemetry into a US console), and nothing covers employer-of-record populations, where the EOR is the legal employer and controls the termination trigger that the entire CS-3 cell depends on.

*Fix.* Add a "Multi-country employment constraints" section to cs-2: the consultation requirement and who owns it, the transparency notice that must accompany enrolment, the data-minimisation choice (device posture yes, content and activity no), the residency question for the management console itself, and the rule to route through people operations and counsel before enrolling any device in an EU jurisdiction. Add an EOR and PEO branch to cs-3 naming the EOR as a required notification path for joiners, movers, and leavers, and recording that a terminated EOR worker is deprovisioned on the EOR's timetable unless a contractual notification term exists.

**M1.6 `references/06-2019-to-2026-delta.md`**

*Problem.* Remote hiring fraud gets one bullet, in the wrong file, with no procedure. Theme 9 names "verifying that the person who interviewed is the person who started" as a legitimate CS-4 item and stops there. For the exact company profile this skill targets, fully remote, contractor-heavy, hiring engineers who receive production access, this is a live high-consequence threat, and the correct response is not a security incident the hire handles alone: it is an HR, legal, and potentially sanctions matter where confronting the person or unilaterally cutting access is the wrong first move. A beginner handed one bullet will either ignore it or handle it badly.

*Fix.* Move it into cs-3-onboarding-offboarding.md as a joiner-side control with an actual procedure: identity verification at offer and confirmation that first login is the same person, a live video check against government ID performed by people operations, device shipped only to the verified address with first enrolment required from it, and the known operational tells (address or payment-detail change after signing, camera refusal, remote-access-tool indicators on the issued device). Add an explicit stop rule: if suspected, do not confront and do not revoke access unilaterally; escalate to the CEO, counsel, and people operations the same day, because sanctions and law-enforcement implications may attach.

**M1.8 `references/05-metrics-and-comms.md`**

*Problem.* The ownership boundary the whole set depends on is never turned into an artifact. Part 7 asserts it ("security owns the standard, the tooling, and the visibility; engineering owns the fix"), tells the hire to say it out loud in month one, and makes it the defence against failure mode 3. DR-4 step 10 correctly calls the written pre-authorisation "the single most valuable sentence you will get in your first 90 days". The founder question bank asks "what am I allowed to change on my own". None of it produces a durable signed document, so the boundary exists only as a remembered conversation and gets relitigated at the first real conflict, which is exactly when it is worth the most.

*Fix.* Add a one-page `SECURITY-CHARTER.md` template to templates/README.md, produced in Gate A and signed by whoever the hire reports to. Fields: what security decides alone, what security recommends and engineering decides, what requires the CEO, the standing incident pre-authorisation using the DR-4 step 10 wording, the risk-acceptance severity ladder from 05 Part 5, the agreed response-time commitment for security reviews, and a review date. Reference it from dr-4 step 10 and from 05 failure mode 3 so neither depends on memory.

**M1.9 `SKILL.md`**

*Problem.* The security program's own data is a concentrated target and is handled inconsistently. SKILL.md and templates/README.md both recommend committing the state directory, including RISK-REGISTER.md, to a private repository. co-3-existing-commitments.md separately and correctly instructs the hire to ask counsel whether the commitment register should be held under privilege, and warns that "we are in breach" language is discoverable in litigation. That argument applies with more force to the risk register, which is a dated, ranked, plain-language list of the company's exploitable weaknesses with named owners. There is also no retention or deletion rule for `evidence/`, which will accumulate IAM dumps, user exports, and console screenshots: a map of weaknesses plus employee personal data sitting on one laptop.

*Fix.* Reconcile the two positions in templates/README.md with a short "protecting this directory" section: ask counsel once in week one whether the risk register and any incident material should be produced under privilege and how to mark it; write findings as neutral factual statements ("control X is not implemented, owner, target date") and never as legal conclusions; set an explicit retention and deletion rule for `evidence/` (delete raw exports once the finding and the command are recorded, keep the finding not the dump); and require full-disk encryption plus the same backup treatment as anything else that matters.

**M1.10 `references/se-4-bug-bounty-and-disclosure.md`**

*Problem.* Penetration testing is named as inevitable 28 times and never made actionable. Founders expect one, questionnaires ask for it by name, CO-2's model answer 11 assumes you have one, this file recommends it as the better purchase than a bounty, CO-1 explains how to share the summary, and DR-1 warns not to run one before the IR plan exists. Nothing anywhere says how to buy one: scoping, choosing a firm, what to give the testers, what the deliverable must contain, retest terms, or how to handle findings you cannot fix before the report is dated and starts circulating to customers. Cyber insurance has the mirror gap: co-3 handles reading an existing policy well, and is silent on the common case where no policy exists and the hire is asked to help buy one, where the application form is a set of security representations they will personally own.

*Fix.* Add a "Buying a penetration test" section to se-4: scope by crown jewel rather than by hostname, fixed-price and time-boxed, require an executive summary suitable for customer distribution plus a separate technical appendix, require a free retest window, test against staging with production-like data, and agree the finding-handling and disclosure process before the report exists. Add a "No policy yet" subsection to co-3 step 6: the application form is a set of representations, security reviews every answer before submission, and any answer that is aspirational rather than current becomes a RISK-REGISTER.md row with the founder named.

**M1.11 `references/05-metrics-and-comms.md`**

*Problem.* The security function's own growth has no decision support. Part 4 covers asking for budget and headcount as a board-slide line, and Part 7 correctly names single-point-of-failure as a failure mode with mechanisation as the defence. But the hire will be asked, usually in the day 90 review, what the function looks like in twelve months, and the set offers nothing: no trigger for a second hire, no comparison of a second engineer against a fractional CISO, an MSSP, a managed detection tier, or a compliance contractor, and no guidance on what to hire first, which differs sharply between a compliance-pressured B2B company and an abuse-pressured B2C one. "MSSP" and "second security hire" appear zero times.

*Fix.* Add a "What comes after you" section expressing headcount triggers in the set's own units: more than roughly four lifecycle events per month (from cs-3), more than roughly ten alerts per week sustained (from dr-2), more than ten questionnaires per quarter (from co-2), an audit committed, or a second cloud or second product. Follow it with a one-paragraph comparison of the four buying options against those triggers with rough cost bands, in the same buy-or-defer format the cells already use. Add the twelve-month org answer to the day 90 review outline in 03-90-day-plan.md, since that meeting is where the question is actually asked.

**M1.12 `references/co-2-questionnaire-knowledge-base.md`**

*Problem.* Customer-facing security features are revenue-blocking work the set never scopes. "Deals unblocked" is one of the seven headline metrics and the strongest budget argument in the entire file set. For a B2B startup the things that actually unblock enterprise deals are frequently product features rather than program controls: customer-facing SSO and SAML, SCIM provisioning, customer-visible audit logs, role-based access control, and IP allowlisting. Model answer 7 acknowledges customer SSO exists and warns about the paid-tier trap, and that is the entire treatment. The hire will be asked to spec and prioritise these and has no guidance, and no way to argue the distinction between security-program work and product work that security sponsors.

*Fix.* Add an "Enterprise readiness features" section listing the five features in the order buyers demand them, what "good enough" means for each at 20 to 100 people, rough engineering cost, and the explicit framing that these belong on the product roadmap with security as the requirements author rather than on the security backlog. Tie prioritisation to the CO-2 request log so the ask is evidenced by counted questionnaire and deal hits rather than by anecdote.

### Minor (2)

**M1.1 `references/cs-1-identity-and-access.md`**

*Problem.* There is no consolidated register of the accounts outside the cloud whose loss ends the company. The domain registrar, the DNS provider, the certificate authority account, the app store developer accounts, the package registry namespace, the payment processor, the payroll and banking portals, and the primary ad and social accounts are each mentioned once or twice in scattered places: the CS-3 "things everyone forgets" list, the phishing-resistant MFA top group in the 2026 delta, and the SE-2 third-party map. Several are typically registered to a founder's personal email with no MFA and no documented recovery path. The SE-2 crown-jewels exercise is framed around systems engineers use, so these never surface there.

*Fix.* Add a named "company-ending accounts" table to the CS-1 door list in step 1, with a fixed row set (registrar, DNS, CA, app stores, package registry namespace, payment processor, payroll, banking, primary ad and social) and three columns that matter: account owner of record, the email address that is the recovery path, and whether a phishing-resistant factor is enrolled with a registered second factor. Make it a Gate A output. It costs an hour and routinely produces the first genuine critical finding.

**M1.2 `templates/README.md`**

*Problem.* There is only one instrument for not doing something: formal risk acceptance, with a severity-based seniority ladder and periods up to twelve months. Engineers need something much lighter and shorter many times a quarter: ship behind a flag with a control missing for two weeks, allowlist an install script, keep a static key alive until the federated path lands. Routing all of those through a CTO signature guarantees they are routed around instead. CS-1's own failure mode "users pile up in the exception group and no entry has an expiry" is exactly this problem observed without the mechanism that would fix it.

*Fix.* Add an `EXCEPTIONS` section to the RISK-REGISTER.md template with a deliberately lower bar: requester, control not met, compensating measure, expiry date of at most 90 days, and approval by the engineering manager rather than an executive. State the promotion rule explicitly: an exception renewed twice becomes a formal risk acceptance at its proper severity, which is what forces the real decision to happen.

## Pass 2: Adversarial accuracy and safety
**Reviewer verdict:** This is a genuinely strong skill: the legal reasoning in co-3, co-4 and se-4 is careful, the cost bands are mostly accurate and hedged, the Danger zone sections are real rather than decorative, and the anti-theater discipline is consistent. The damage is concentrated in three places. First, three items are dangerous if followed literally: a copy-pasteable message that makes a false claim to a CTO about what AWS ReadOnlyAccess and GCP roles/viewer grant, a founder pre-authorisation template in dr-4 that grants blanket permission to revoke any credential and disable any service account without approval (contradicting every hard stop in the skill), and dr-1's fast-path instruction not to rotate keys until memory is captured, which extends an active compromise at a company with no forensics capability. Second, the Salesloft Drift remediation claim is causally wrong and repeated in five files, so a reader ships the wrong control and reports false coverage. Third, templates/README.md is out of sync with the reference files on almost every structural convention it defines: modern-cell identifiers, CONTEXT-STACK format and frame ceiling, gate names and day ranges, and the unknown-versus-partial honesty rule that is the skill's own load-bearing principle. Beyond that, roughly a dozen commands are wrong or silently return nothing (the async credential report in four files, `grep -c '^\s' go.sum`, `tr '\n' '\n'`, the `{org}` placeholder, the 403-as-no-protection conflation), and two discovery greps print live API keys into the transcript in direct violation of the skill's own no-secret-values rule. There are 22 em dashes across three files and no AI attribution anywhere.
