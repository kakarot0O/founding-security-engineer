# Generating a 90 day plan from findings

> **Load when:** the human asks "what do I do first", "give me a 90 day plan", "what's next", "am I on track", or when the cold start completion checklist in `references/00-cold-start.md` is fully ticked and recon (`references/01-recon.md`) has produced enough facts to sequence work. Also load when a discovered fact (a live incident, a contractual commitment, a blocked deal) means the existing `90-DAY-PLAN.md` needs to be regenerated.

## Rule 0: findings drive the plan, this document never does

This file is a method for building a plan out of what you found in a specific company. It is not a plan. Nothing in it is scheduled work until a fact about this company makes it scheduled work.

The gates, the candidate steps, and the step identifiers below exist so that you, the agent, do not forget an entire domain while you are enjoying yourself in the codebase. They are a checklist for your blind spots. They are not a work order for the human.

Four consequences you must respect in every turn:

1. **Never justify a step by its position in this document.** "Identity and access is next because it is second in Gate A" is banned. "Identity and access is next because four people hold super administrator rights in the identity provider and two of them left the company last year" is required. If you cannot name the fact, you do not have the step.
2. **A cell can be closed as not applicable, with a written reason.** Filling in every row is completionism, and completionism will eat the quarter. Write "n/a, verified on <date>, because <reason>" into `SECURITY-STATE.md` and move on. That is a finished cell, not a skipped one.
3. **A finding that fits no cell is still real.** If recon turns up something that does not belong to any of the domains below, work it anyway and record it. The grid does not get a veto over reality.
4. **A step with no finding attached does not go in the file.** `90-DAY-PLAN.md` and the gate tables in `templates/README.md` carry only the steps that findings have actually justified, each written with its identifier and the finding that justified it. An empty gate table is the correct state until a finding puts a step in it, and it is a better state than four plausible steps nobody has evidence for. The identifiers below are illustrative; they are not a starting set to be copied across.

Corollary for how you speak: the cell identifiers (SE-2, CS-1, DR-0 and the rest) and the step identifiers (GA-01 and so on) are internal bookkeeping. Use them in state files and in your own reasoning. Do not narrate them at the human turn by turn. The human should hear "let's find out who can log into the cloud account", not "next up is CS-1".

## The single most important rule about disclosure

Do not show the human this plan.

You are holding a map. The human is holding a flashlight and standing in a dark room. If you read the whole map out loud, they will freeze, they will feel behind on day 3, and they will start six things and finish none. First security hires do not fail because they picked the wrong project. They fail because they picked eleven projects.

So: you walk one step at a time. You name the current step, you get a go or no-go, you do the step, you verify it, you write it to state, and only then do you name the next step. The human is allowed to ask "what does the whole quarter look like", and when they do you may give them the four gate objectives (four sentences, one per gate) and nothing more. You do not hand over the step list. The step list is yours, and in any case the step list is not real until findings have populated it.

If you catch yourself writing a numbered list longer than five items for the human to read, stop. That is the failure mode.

## How to turn a finding into a step

This is the core loop of the file. Run it every time you need a next step.

1. **Re-read the facts.** Open `SECURITY-STATE.md` and `RISK-REGISTER.md`. If either is thin, the honest next step is more recon, not more plan.
2. **Rank the open findings by damage and by reversibility.** Damage first: what would this cost the company if it went wrong tomorrow. Then reversibility: a finding that gets permanently worse with time (an expiring log window, a laptop fleet that grows every week, a credential that is already public) outranks an equally damaging finding that will wait patiently.
3. **Check for a hard deadline attached to a fact.** A contractual notification window, an audit date, a fundraise timeline, a customer call already booked. Deadlines beat rankings.
4. **Check the dependency.** Some steps are worthless without another one first. You cannot rank a secrets finding without knowing where code runs. You cannot alert on a log you do not collect. If the chosen step has an unmet dependency, the dependency becomes the step.
5. **Name the gate the step belongs to** so you can tell whether the quarter is drifting. This is bookkeeping, not sequencing. A Gate C step is allowed to happen on day 6 if a finding demands it.
6. **Write the step in the five-part format below and stop talking.**

If step 2 produces nothing, that is a real answer. It means the highest value activity is more discovery, or it means a domain you have not touched is where the unknowns live. Look at which rows of `SECURITY-STATE.md` still say unknown, and go find out. That is the only legitimate use of the grid as a prompt: it tells you where you are blind, not what to build.

## The per-step gate format (use this exact shape every time)

Every step you propose to the human uses this five-part format. Keep it under about 150 words. Write it as prose and short lines, not as a wall of bullets.

1. **The step.** One sentence, concrete, in the imperative. "Pull the full list of administrator accounts in your identity provider and confirm every one of them by name." Not "improve identity hygiene."
2. **Why now.** One or two plain sentences connecting this step to something you actually discovered, or to a dependency. This sentence must contain a fact about this company. "You told me sales promised a SOC 2 report (System and Organization Controls 2, an audited report on a service company's controls) by the third quarter. Until I know what was signed, every other priority I give you might be wrong."
3. **Time cost.** An honest range in the human's time, plus who else it pulls in. "About 40 minutes of your time, plus a 15 minute conversation with whoever owns the identity provider."
4. **What could go wrong.** Name the real risk, including political risk. "The list will probably contain a founder who does not want to lose administrator rights. Do not remove anyone in this step. This step only produces a list."
5. **The ask.** A closed question. "Go or no-go?"

Then stop and wait. Do not begin the step, do not pre-fetch, do not "start while we discuss." A no-go is a legitimate answer and you record it in `DECISION-LOG.md` with the reason.

If the human says go, execute the step, then close it with a verification statement: what you ran or what they confirmed, what the result was, and which line of `SECURITY-STATE.md` you just updated. Then propose exactly one next step in the same format.

**Approval is per action, never per batch.** Any step that changes access, roles, or authentication requirements for anyone; enforces multi-factor authentication (a second login factor beyond the password), conditional access, or device compliance on a group of people; enrols or wipes a device; rotates a credential that production uses; runs any active scan or test against any system, including systems the company owns, without written authorisation; touches a customer or customer data; publishes anything externally; commits to a customer that a control exists or will exist by a date; enables a log source that could materially increase a bill; deletes, force-pushes, or rewrites repository history; or spends money, requires a fresh explicit yes from a named human with the authority to give it, recorded in `DECISION-LOG.md`. "You approved the identity cleanup" is not consent to remove a specific founder's administrator rights.

## The step identifier scheme

Steps get stable identifiers so `90-DAY-PLAN.md`, `DECISION-LOG.md`, and `CONTEXT-STACK.md` can refer to the same thing. The format is `GA-01`, `GB-01`, `GC-01`, `GD-01`, where the letter is the gate and the number is allocated in the order you first wrote the step down for this company.

The number is an allocation order, not a running order. `GA-05` running before `GA-02` is normal and requires no explanation. Identifiers are never reused. A step that is dropped keeps its identifier and gets a status of dropped with a reason.

The candidate steps listed under each gate below are a menu, and their identifiers in this document are illustrative. When you generate `90-DAY-PLAN.md` for a real company, you allocate identifiers to the steps that the findings actually justified, and the numbering will not match this file. That is correct.

## Why four gates and not three months

Calendar months are a reporting convenience, not a security sequence. Two problems with "month one, month two, month three": it implies the work is evenly sized (it is not, understanding is fast and durability is slow), and it implies you cannot start month two work until month one ends (you must, because some of it is bleeding right now).

So the plan is four gates defined by exit criteria, not by dates. The day ranges are advisory.

| Gate | Name | Advisory days | Objective in one sentence |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Understand | 1 to 14 | Know what exists, who has access to it, whether someone is already inside, and what has already been promised to customers, well enough that your next decision is informed rather than guessed. |
| B | Stop the bleeding | 10 to 30 | Close the small number of findings that would turn a bad day into a company-ending day, without breaking anything or anyone. |
| C | Build the floor | 30 to 60 | Put in place the minimum durable controls and processes that keep the floor from rotting the moment you look away. |
| D | Make it durable and prove it | 60 to 90 | Turn the controls into things that run without you, and produce evidence a founder, a customer, or an auditor will accept. |

**Overlap is intentional and you should say so out loud.** Gate A runs to day 14, Gate B starts around day 10. That is not sloppiness. During Gate A you will find at least one thing that is on fire (a live production credential in a public place, a former employee who still has access, an administrator account with no second factor). You do not finish understanding before you put that out. The rule is: Gate A work continues in the background while a Gate B fix happens in the foreground, and you return to Gate A the moment the fix is verified. The interrupt protocol in `references/04-interrupts.md` governs that switch, including pushing the parked Gate A step onto `CONTEXT-STACK.md`.

The gates are also honest about the source material. Evan Johnson's framing of this grid at OWASP AppSec California 2019 was that the cells are deliberately short in scope: you can get roughly 95 percent of the way to initially addressing all of them in a quarter. "Initially addressing" is the operative phrase. Ninety days gets you a floor, not a program. And 95 percent of the things that matter here beats 100 percent of two of them.

## Exit criteria are authoritative here

The exit criteria written under each gate below are the authoritative definition of what finishing that gate means. `templates/README.md` defers to this file: if a template's checklist and this file disagree about what a gate requires, this file wins and the template should be corrected.

Exit criteria are not a to-do list either. They are a test. A criterion that is not met at the end of a gate has exactly two legitimate dispositions: move it forward explicitly with a written reason, or open it as a risk in `RISK-REGISTER.md` with an owner and a review date. Silently dropping a criterion is not allowed. Neither is stalling a gate transition for a criterion that a finding has made irrelevant, in which case you mark it not applicable with the reason and move on.

## Gate A: Understand (days 1 to 14)

**Objective.** Know what exists, who has access to it, whether someone is already inside, and what has already been promised to customers, well enough that your next decision is informed rather than guessed.

**Candidate steps, and the finding that would put each one in front of you.** These are a menu. Pick from it based on `SECURITY-STATE.md`, not based on the order they appear here.

- **GA-01, map the technology stack (SE-2).** Justified when you cannot yet name every place code runs and every store that holds customer data. This is usually true on day one, which is why it is usually early, but the justification is the ignorance, not the position. Note the honest caveat from the original talk: the deep way to learn a stack is to build software in it, and if you were hired as a head of security rather than as an engineer, that is probably not the best use of your first quarter. In Gate A you are doing the shallow version: an inventory and a diagram, not a code contribution. See `references/se-2-understand-the-tech-stack.md`.
- **GA-02, enumerate identity and administrator rights (CS-1).** Justified when nobody can tell you how many people hold administrator rights in the identity provider, the cloud account, or the source code host. Identity is the front door to every software-as-a-service tool, every cloud console, and increasingly to production itself. The original talk's line still holds and is worth repeating to founders verbatim: production takes years to fix, corporate takes a few quarters. In 2026 this is stronger, not weaker, because the dominant startup breach path now starts on a laptop or in a browser session and moves through software-as-a-service applications into cloud. This step produces a list and changes nothing. See `references/cs-1-identity-and-access.md` and `references/06-2019-to-2026-delta.md`.
- **GA-03, read the security terms in signed customer contracts (CO-3).** Justified when the company sells to other businesses and nobody has read the security clauses since they were signed. This is the highest-variance discovery available to you and the one that most often reorders everything else. If a signed master services agreement says you will notify customers of a security incident within 24 hours, your incident response plan is not a Gate C item, it is urgent. If a contract promised a SOC 2 Type 2 report by a date that is eight weeks away, the quarter has a different purpose than you thought. See `references/co-3-existing-commitments.md`.
- **GA-04, establish log retention horizons (DR-0 precondition).** Justified always, and it is time critical, which is why it deserves attention in the first week even when nothing looks wrong. For each log source you can name (identity provider sign-ins, cloud control plane audit logs, source code host audit logs, third-party application grant logs, email and chat audit logs), write down the retention period and therefore the calendar date on which the oldest evidence disappears. Many default retentions are short: some identity and software-as-a-service audit logs default to 30, 90, or 180 days, and some are shorter on lower-tier plans. Once you know the dates, you know how long you have to look backwards. Extending a retention period usually costs money and is therefore an explicit approval, not something you do quietly.
- **GA-05, run a retrospective compromise assessment (DR-0).** Justified when the answer to "has anyone already been in here" is unknown, which it is at every company that has never had a security hire. This is a read-only review of existing logs and configuration for signs of a compromise that already happened. It is not a penetration test and not an active scan, and it must not become one. It is placed in Gate A rather than later purely because the evidence expires: the log windows you measured in GA-04 are counting down, and a compromise assessment run in month three can only see month three. If GA-05 finds something, you stop planning and go to `references/dr-1-incident-response-plan.md`, and everything else in this document waits. See `references/dr-0-compromise-assessment.md`.
- **GA-06, inventory the modern surfaces.** Justified when recon surfaced a build pipeline, cloud accounts, third-party application grants connected to the identity provider, or artificial intelligence and large language model features in the product. You are not fixing these in Gate A, you are writing them down. `references/07-modern-cells.md` covers why these are now first-class rather than optional.
- **GA-07, ask the person who hired you what they hired you for.** Justified always. Write their answer down verbatim in `DECISION-LOG.md`. It will differ from what you think the job is, and that gap is the single best predictor of whether you get renewed budget.
- **GA-08, pull data classification forward (CO-4).** Justified only when a regulated data class may be present: health information, payment card data, children's data, biometric data, or data from a regulated sector. When that is true, this stops being a Gate D item because the legal obligations attach whether or not anyone knows about them. See `references/co-4-data-inventory-and-framework.md`.
- **GA-09, agree the decision rights charter.** Justified when the hire cannot state in one sentence what they decide alone, which is true at almost every company on day one and stays true until somebody writes it down. This is program-level rather than a grid cell. The artifact is one page, `SECURITY-CHARTER.md` in the state directory, agreed and signed by whoever the human reports to, covering: what security decides alone, what security recommends while engineering decides, what needs the chief executive, the standing incident containment pre-authorisation in the exact wording of step 10 of `references/dr-4-company-comms-channel.md`, the risk acceptance severity ladder, the response time commitment for security reviews, and the date the whole thing is reviewed again. It sits in Gate A rather than later for two reasons. A boundary that exists only as a remembered conversation gets relitigated at the first real conflict, which is precisely when it is worth the most. And the containment pre-authorisation it carries is worth nothing if it is first raised during an incident, which is the only time it is ever needed. Record the date it was agreed, and by whom, in `DECISION-LOG.md`. The field list is the `SECURITY-CHARTER.md` template in `templates/README.md`, and `references/05-metrics-and-comms.md` holds the severity ladder in Part 5 and the reasoning in Part 7.

**Exit criteria (all must be demonstrably true, with evidence recorded, or explicitly dispositioned).**

- A one page architecture diagram exists that names every data store and marks which ones hold customer data. Evidence: the file, and one engineer who has looked at it and said it is right.
- A list of every identity provider, cloud account, source code host, and chat platform in use, with the named human who administers each. Evidence: the list, plus at least one screenshot or read-only command output per platform confirming you can see it.
- A count of administrators per platform, with each administrator identified by name and role, and the count of those lacking phishing resistant or application-based multi-factor authentication. Evidence: exported user list or console screenshot.
- A written log retention table naming each source, its retention period, and the date its oldest evidence expires. Evidence: the table, with the console page or command that produced each number.
- A compromise assessment has been run against the sources that were available, and its result is recorded as one of: no indicators found, indicators found and escalated to incident response, or not performed with a written reason. Evidence: the assessment notes, including which sources were and were not searchable and why.
- The security-relevant contents of the top five customer contracts by revenue are summarized in `SECURITY-STATE.md` under the commitments row, specifically: notification windows, certification promises, audit rights, subprocessor terms, and data residency terms. Evidence: the summary plus who provided the contracts. For a company with no customer contracts, this is marked not applicable with the reason.
- Every grid row in `SECURITY-STATE.md` has a status of unknown, none, partial, done, or n/a. No row is blank. Unknown is a legitimate and useful status, and n/a requires a written reason.
- At least the top five risks are in `RISK-REGISTER.md` with a severity and a named owner, even if the owner is a founder and the decision is "not yet."

**Artifacts produced.** `SECURITY-STATE.md` populated across the grid. `RISK-REGISTER.md` seeded. `ACCESS-LOG.md` showing what access you asked for, from whom, and whether it was granted. The architecture diagram. The log retention table. The compromise assessment notes. The contract commitments summary in `COMMITMENT-REGISTER.md` when there are enough commitments to justify the file. `SECURITY-CHARTER.md`, one page, signed and dated, when GA-09 was justified.

**A note on the access you request in this gate.** Ask for the smallest role that answers the question. For Amazon Web Services, the correct minimal pair is the SecurityAudit policy plus ViewOnlyAccess. Do not ask for ReadOnlyAccess and describe it as harmless: it includes `s3:Get*` and `dynamodb:Scan`, so it grants bulk read of customer data. For Google Cloud Platform, ask for `roles/iam.securityReviewer` plus `roles/browser` plus specific service viewer roles as needed. Do not ask for `roles/viewer` and describe it as metadata only: it includes `storage.objects.get`, so it too reads customer data. For Microsoft Azure, ask for Reader plus Security Reader; Azure Reader alone is genuinely control plane only and does not read data plane contents. Say which of these you are asking for and why, and record the grant in `ACCESS-LOG.md`.

**Meetings and conversations that must happen.**

- Thirty minutes with each of: the person who deploys most often, the person who owns the cloud account, the person who owns the identity provider, and the head of sales or the person who answers customer security questions. Question banks are in `references/02-intake-questions.md`.
- One conversation with whoever signs contracts (a founder, a head of revenue, or outside counsel) about existing commitments.
- One conversation with the person who hired you about what they think they hired you for.

**The trap that kills people in this gate.** Auditing instead of listening. New security hires open a scanner, generate 4,000 findings, and present them in week two. This is career damage: it tells the engineering organisation that you produce work for them rather than with them, and none of the findings are prioritized because you do not yet know what matters. Note also that running a scanner against anything is an active test and needs written authorisation before it happens, even against systems the company owns. If you do run one with authorisation, keep the output in your own state files and present nothing from it until Gate B, when you can rank it. The second version of this trap is the opposite: being so agreeable in week one that you never ask for access, and arriving at day 30 still blind. `ACCESS-LOG.md` exists to make the second failure visible.

## Gate B: Stop the bleeding (days 10 to 30)

**Objective.** Close the small number of findings that would turn a bad day into a company-ending day, without breaking anything or anyone.

**Candidate steps, and the finding that would put each one in front of you.**

- **GB-01, secrets and keys (SE-3).** Justified when Gate A found long-lived credentials in places that cannot rotate them safely: on laptops, in a repository's history, in a build pipeline's static configuration, in a shared password note. A leaked long-lived credential is the shortest path from "someone found something" to "customer data is gone," and most fixes are quick and reversible. The original talk's instinct still applies: take inventory of the critical assets, and be genuinely alarmed if engineers hold long-lived cloud application programming interface keys on their laptops. The 2026 update is that the same credential class now sits in continuous integration, which has no human at the keyboard, so the highest-value single change is often replacing static cloud keys in the build pipeline with short-lived federated identity. Every rotation of a credential production uses is an explicit approval with a named approver and a stated rollback. See `references/se-3-secrets-and-keys.md`.
- **GB-02, a communication channel with the rest of the company (DR-4).** Justified as soon as you are about to change anything that affects other people, which in practice is now. It is cheap (a chat channel, an email alias, a one-paragraph announcement) and it is a dependency for everything else in this gate. Before you start removing access and changing defaults, people need a place to ask "did you do this to my account" and a place to report "this looks weird." Without it, your Gate B changes generate confusion that gets attributed to you personally. See `references/dr-4-company-comms-channel.md`.
- **GB-03, close the identity gaps Gate A found (CS-1).** Justified by specific named accounts, not by a general sense that identity could be better. Typical items: multi-factor authentication on every administrator, removal of accounts belonging to people who have left, and disabling of the setting that lets any employee grant a third-party application access to company data. Each of these changes someone's access or authentication requirements and therefore requires its own explicit approval from a named human, one action at a time. Never batch. Have the private conversation with any individual who is about to lose access before they lose it, never after.
- **GB-04, whatever the commitment review turned up as already overdue (CO-3).** Justified when a contract promised something and the date has passed or is close. It belongs here rather than later because it is both a security risk and a legal one, and because the founders, not you, need to decide what to do about it.
- **GB-05, consumer account security emergency items (SE-5).** Justified only when the company sells to consumers and Gate A found something acutely wrong in the account lifecycle: no rate limiting on login, password reset tokens that do not expire, account recovery that a support agent can perform on a phone call with no verification, or credential stuffing that is already happening and nobody noticed. For a business-to-consumer company these items outrank most of the compliance domain, because the consumer account is the product's front door and there is no procurement process standing in front of it. See `references/se-5-consumer-account-security.md`.

**The absolute rule for this gate.** Every change in Gate B can lock a person out, break a deploy, cost money, or notify a customer. Therefore every change in Gate B follows the same pattern: state a STOP, describe exactly what will change and who it affects, name the rollback, get an explicit yes from a named human who has the authority to approve it, then make the change, then verify it, then record the approver in `DECISION-LOG.md`.

**Exit criteria.**

- Zero long-lived cloud credentials in the build pipeline, or a dated, owner-assigned, founder-acknowledged exception in `RISK-REGISTER.md` explaining why not. Evidence: the pipeline configuration showing federated or short-lived credentials, and confirmation that the old keys were deleted rather than merely unused.
- Every administrator on every platform has a second factor, verified by looking at the platform's own report, not by asking people.
- Every departed employee identified in Gate A is confirmed removed from every platform in the inventory. Evidence: per-platform user list showing absence.
- A security reporting channel exists, has been announced company-wide once, and has at least one message in it. Evidence: the announcement text in `DECISION-LOG.md`.
- Secret scanning is enabled on the source code host (this is free on the major hosts for many plan types, and free to self-run otherwise), and the historical findings have been triaged into rotate now, rotate later, and false positive.
- Every risk you decided not to fix has an explicit acceptance in `RISK-REGISTER.md` with an accepted-by name and a date. Silence is not acceptance.

**Artifacts produced.** A written secrets inventory. A rotation record showing what was rotated and when. The company announcement. An updated `RISK-REGISTER.md` where the top items now have decisions rather than just severities.

**Meetings and conversations.** One short all-hands or all-company chat post introducing yourself and the reporting channel, in plain language, with no security jargon and no threats. One working session with whoever owns the build pipeline. One private conversation with any individual who is about to lose access, before they lose it.

**The trap that kills people in this gate.** Moving fast on the wrong axis. The instinct is to fix everything you found, immediately, because it is all bad. Two things go wrong. First, you break a deploy on day 18 and spend your remaining political capital apologizing. Second, you rotate a credential without knowing every consumer of it and take down a service. The discipline: for each fix, ask "what reads this credential" and "who loses access" before touching it, and prefer additive changes (add the new credential path, verify it works, then remove the old one) over destructive ones. The related trap is the cleanup that quietly removes a founder's access without a conversation. That single act has ended more first-security-hire tenures than any breach.

## Gate C: Build the floor (days 30 to 60)

**Objective.** Put in place the minimum durable controls and processes that keep the floor from rotting the moment you look away.

**Candidate steps, and the finding that would put each one in front of you.**

- **GC-01, onboarding and offboarding (CS-3).** Justified when the Gate B identity cleanup found people who should have been removed months ago, which means the process, not the list, is the defect. If you clean up access and do not fix the joiner and leaver process, you are back where you started in two hiring cycles. The original talk is explicit that onboarding and offboarding is where so much security is baked in and is tightly coupled to identity and access management. It is also the cheapest possible durability: a checklist, a group-based access model, and a single owner in the people operations function. See `references/cs-3-onboarding-offboarding.md`.
- **GC-02, endpoint baseline (CS-2).** Justified when Gate A could not answer "is this laptop ours and is it patched" for the fleet. Johnson marked this cell on the original slide with a gift emoji, which was his marker for a cheap win: high value for low effort. That is a hint about effort, not about importance. The effort argument is real and it decays: rolling out device management to 25 laptops is a week, rolling it out to 250 is a quarter and a fight. Minimum viable endpoint in 2026 is disk encryption on, screen lock on, operating system updates enforced, and enough visibility to answer the ownership and patch question. Enrolling anyone's device is an explicit approval and a communication, never a silent push. Keep the fleet list in `devices.csv`. See `references/cs-2-endpoint-security.md`.
- **GC-03, a basic incident response plan (DR-1).** Justified once you know enough to write one that is true, which is usually after the stack map, the identity picture, and the contractual clocks exist. Writing an incident response plan in week one produces a document that names systems that do not exist and promises notification windows that contradict the contracts. Writing it here produces a plan you can actually run. Keep it to two pages: who declares, who leads, where you coordinate, who talks to customers, and the contractual clocks. See `references/dr-1-incident-response-plan.md`.
- **GC-04, the logging consumption model (DR-3).** Justified before any new log source is turned on, because a team that enables everything without a consumption model gets a five figure monthly bill and an unqueryable pile. Decide where logs land, who can query them, how long they are kept, and what it costs. Enabling a log source that could materially increase the bill is an explicit approval with the number said out loud to whoever owns that bill. See `references/dr-3-logging-consumption-model.md`.
- **GC-05, the small number of signals worth alerting on (DR-2).** Justified once logs have somewhere to land and a person to receive an alert. The original talk's concrete starting set still works, translated to whatever the company actually uses: cloud application programming interface key usage, identity provider sign-ins and application access, and corporate network or domain name resolution. Add one that did not exist in 2019: changes to build pipeline configuration and to who can publish or deploy. Accept openly that detection and response is the hardest area to get traction in and that you will not have complete coverage. See `references/dr-2-top-security-signals.md`.
- **GC-06, design reviews, introduced gradually (SE-1).** Justified when engineers start bringing you things, or when a specific high-risk feature is being built. In Gate C you are running ad hoc, inbound, opt-in reviews on two or three real features, doing them fast and usefully, and letting word spread. You are not publishing a mandatory process. Record what you learn about the delivery process in `sdlc-map.md`. See `references/se-1-sdlc-and-design-reviews.md` and the reasoning under "Why design review is rarely first" below.
- **GC-07, backup inventory (M-6).** Justified when nobody can answer, from memory, which systems have backups. The question is not "do we have backups", to which the answer is always yes. The questions are: which data stores are backed up, to where, on what schedule, who holds the credentials that could delete both the primary and the backup, and when was a restore last performed. Most startups discover that the answer to the last one is never. See `references/m-6-backups-and-recovery.md`.
- **GC-08, one real restore test (M-6).** Justified as soon as GC-07 shows a backup that nobody has ever restored. A backup that has never been restored is a belief, not a control. Restore one system that matters into an isolated location, time it, and write down both the time and everything that went wrong. This is a mutating action in the sense that it creates infrastructure and can cost money, so it needs an explicit approval, and it must restore to a separate location rather than over anything live. Never restore over production to prove a point.
- **GC-09, backup blast radius (M-6).** Justified when GC-07 shows that the same credential, account, or console session that can delete the production database can also delete its backups. The modern extortion pattern deletes backups first. The fix is separation and immutability: backups in a different account or project or subscription, delete protection or object lock or immutability enabled, and a credential path to the backups that day-to-day production does not have. Every one of those changes is an explicit approval, and several of them cost money. **The one documented jump.** GC-09 moves to Gate B when the answer to "can the identity that runs production also delete the backups" is yes. That single answer decides it and nothing else about backups does: GC-07 and GC-08 stay in Gate C either way, and a yes on that one question means a single compromised credential ends the company on a Tuesday afternoon, which is the definition of Gate B. This file owns gate assignment for M-6, so if `references/m-6-backups-and-recovery.md` or `SKILL.md` appears to say something different, this line wins.

**Exit criteria.**

- A written onboarding checklist and an offboarding checklist exist, are owned by a named non-security person, and have been executed at least once for a real joiner or leaver with you observing.
- Every company-owned laptop is enrolled in whatever management exists, or there is a dated list of the exceptions with names against them, recorded in `devices.csv`.
- A two page incident response plan exists, names people rather than roles that do not exist, includes the contractual notification clocks, and has been read aloud in one meeting with the founders and the on-call engineering lead.
- Logs from at least the identity provider, the cloud control plane, and the source code host land somewhere queryable, with a written retention period and a known monthly cost.
- At least three alerts exist that a named human receives, and each one has been deliberately triggered once in a test so you know it fires and know who it reaches.
- At least two real design reviews have been completed, each producing written notes and at least one change the engineering team accepted. For a company with no in-house engineering, this is marked not applicable with the reason and replaced by the vendor equivalent in `references/09-outsourced-engineering.md`.
- A backup inventory exists naming every data store that holds customer data, whether it is backed up, to where, and who can delete both copies. At least one restore has been performed and timed, or the absence of a tested restore is an accepted risk in `RISK-REGISTER.md` with a named accepter and a date.

**Artifacts produced.** The two checklists. `devices.csv`. The incident response plan. A one page logging design including retention and cost. A written alert list with owners. Two sets of design review notes. The backup inventory and the restore test record. `SECURITY-STATE.md` moves several rows from unknown or none to partial or done.

**Meetings and conversations.** A working session with people operations or whoever owns hiring, because onboarding and offboarding is their process and you are a contributor, not the owner. A tabletop walkthrough of the incident response plan, ninety minutes, with the founders in the room. A recurring fifteen minute slot with the engineering lead, which is how design reviews stop being an interruption and start being a habit.

**The trap that kills people in this gate.** Building a process nobody asked for and nobody runs. The tell is a document with more than five steps that only you have ever opened. The other trap is logging cost: turning on verbose logging across a cloud account without a consumption model can produce a bill that gets security's budget frozen for a year, and that is a self-inflicted wound you will not recover from quickly. Get a cost estimate before enabling anything, and say the number out loud to whoever owns the bill.

## Gate D: Make it durable and prove it (days 60 to 90)

**Objective.** Turn the controls into things that run without you, and produce evidence a founder, a customer, or an auditor will accept.

**Candidate steps, and the finding that would put each one in front of you.**

- **GD-01, public facing security documentation (CO-1).** Justified when customers or prospects are asking questions that a page could answer, or when there is no route for an outside researcher to report a vulnerability. A security page describing how you protect data, a contact address for reporting vulnerabilities, and a plain statement of what you do and do not do today. It converts work you already did into revenue support, which is the fastest way to make security legible to founders. It is also the honest artifact: if writing it makes you uncomfortable, that discomfort is a finding. Publishing anything externally is an explicit approval, and the claims on the page must be true on the day it goes live. See `references/co-1-public-security-docs.md`.
- **GD-02, the questionnaire knowledge base (CO-2).** Justified when at least two real security questionnaires have arrived. Not before. The original talk is blunt and correct: your highest and best use is not completing questionnaires for sales, and if you spend all of your time on them you will not make real progress. The fix is not refusing them, it is making them self-service. Build `QUESTIONNAIRE-KB.md` keyed to the questions you have actually been asked, track the incoming ones in `QUESTIONNAIRE-LOG.md`, put the answer bank where sales can reach it, and require that they attempt it before escalating to you. See `references/co-2-questionnaire-knowledge-base.md`.
- **GD-03, data inventory, privacy commitments, and framework choice (CO-4).** Justified when a framework decision is being pushed for, when a privacy commitment exists in a contract or a public policy, or when nobody can say what data classes the company holds. Know what data classes you hold, where each lives, who can read each, how long you keep it, and which subprocessors touch it. Then, and only then, choose a compliance framework, because the framework choice should follow the data and the sales motion rather than leading it. Framework selection made in ignorance of the data inventory is how startups end up paying for the wrong audit. On the original slide this cell was blank, and Johnson filled it in on later slides as privacy regulation and current laws; the version here is a superset of that answer rather than an invention filling a void. See `references/co-4-data-inventory-and-framework.md`.
- **GD-04, workplace security sized to reality (CS-4).** Justified in proportion to how much physical surface the company actually has. For a fully remote company this may be twenty minutes of work: shipping and disposal of hardware, and a note about home network and physical device handling. For a company with an office holding servers or badge systems, it is more. Do not import an enterprise physical security program into a fifteen person company. See `references/cs-4-workplace-security.md`.
- **GD-05, the evidence pass.** Justified always, before the day 90 review. Walk every row in `SECURITY-STATE.md` marked done and confirm it has an evidence line. Any row that cannot produce one goes back to partial. This is the step that makes the day 90 review survivable.
- **GD-06, regenerate the plan for the next quarter.** The last step is producing the next 90 days, informed by everything you now know rather than by what you guessed on day 1.

**Exit criteria.**

- A public security page and a vulnerability reporting address exist, are linked from the main website, and route to a mailbox someone reads. For a company where a public page is not the right artifact, this is marked not applicable with the reason and replaced by whatever the equivalent is for that audience.
- A questionnaire answer bank exists with at least the questions from one real completed questionnaire, and at least one questionnaire has been completed by someone other than you using it. For a company that has never received a questionnaire, this is marked not applicable with the reason.
- A data inventory exists listing data classes, storage locations, access, retention, and subprocessors. It does not need to be complete. It needs to be true about the customer data.
- A framework decision is recorded in `DECISION-LOG.md` with the reasoning, including the option of "not yet, and here is what would change that."
- Every control claimed as done in `SECURITY-STATE.md` has an evidence line: a command, an export, a screenshot the human confirmed, or a named person who verified it.
- The day 90 review has been delivered to the founders, and the next quarter's plan exists in `90-DAY-PLAN.md`.

**Artifacts produced.** The public security page. The vulnerability disclosure contact and a one paragraph policy. `QUESTIONNAIRE-KB.md`. The data inventory. The framework decision record. The day 90 review material. A regenerated `90-DAY-PLAN.md`.

**Meetings and conversations.** One conversation with sales leadership about the new questionnaire process, framed as speed for them rather than as work avoidance by you. One conversation with the founders about framework choice, with a cost number attached. The day 90 review itself.

**The trap that kills people in this gate.** Certification theater. There is enormous pull toward starting a SOC 2 or ISO 27001 (an international standard for information security management systems) audit in month three because it is legible and a vendor will happily sell it. If the underlying controls are not real, the audit produces a document that is true on paper and a program that is hollow, plus a recurring cost that crowds out the actual work. The other trap is declaring something done without evidence. If you cannot show it, it is not done, and the first customer with a real security team will find that out in a call where you are the person who has to answer.

## Defending an ordering once findings have produced one

When the findings do produce an ordering that looks like the common one, you will be asked to defend it. Here is the reasoning, so you can. Note that every argument below is an argument about dependency, decay, or leverage. None of them is an argument from position in a list, and if you ever catch yourself making one, you have lost the thread.

**Stack understanding and identity tend to come first because everything downstream is a guess without them.** The stack map tells you what exists, the identity picture tells you who can reach it. They also differ usefully in speed: the stack map is slow and never really finishes, identity produces a visible win in days. Running them together gives the human both momentum and grounding. Identity leads on impact because a single control change (second factor everywhere, administrator count reduced from eleven to three) removes entire attack paths at zero cost. In 2026 this is more true than in 2019, because the common startup compromise chain now runs through a stolen browser session or an over-permissioned third-party application grant rather than through a network intrusion.

**Commitment review comes early because it is the highest-variance discovery available.** It is the only area whose findings can invalidate the whole plan. Discovering in week two that a contract promises 24 hour breach notification moves the incident response plan forward by a month. Discovering that someone promised a certification by a near date changes what the quarter is for. Leaving this to month three is the single most common sequencing mistake, and it is unrecoverable when it goes wrong.

**Compromise assessment is early because the evidence expires.** This is the only argument for its position and it is sufficient on its own. Log retention windows are counting down from the day you arrive, and on many default plans they are 30 to 180 days. Every week you wait is a week of history you can never get back. It is also the only step whose negative result is genuinely valuable: "we looked and found nothing, here is what we could and could not see" is a real finding that changes how you read everything else.

**Secrets and the company channel tend to come next.** Secrets because leaked credentials are the shortest path to catastrophe and most fixes are fast. The company channel because it is nearly free and is a prerequisite for making changes that affect other people without generating suspicion. The channel is placed unusually early relative to the rest of its domain on purpose: the rest of detection and response is slow, but the channel is not, and it pays for itself the first time someone reports a phishing email instead of clicking it.

**Joiner and leaver process follows the identity cleanup rather than preceding it,** because it is the thing that makes the cleanup permanent, and because you cannot design the process well until you have seen exactly how the current one fails. Endpoint work sits alongside it because it is table stakes with a rising cost curve: every month you wait adds laptops and adds people who will resent the change. It is usually not in Gate B because it is not usually bleeding, and because it requires budget conversations that go better once you have shown a win.

**Plan before pipes before alerts.** The incident response plan needs the contractual clocks and the real system names to be true. The consumption model needs to exist before log volume does, or the cost surprises you. Signals come last because a signal without a place to land and a person to receive it is not a signal, it is a notification nobody reads. Be honest with the human that this domain feels unrewarding for a long time and then becomes suddenly valuable, which is exactly what the original talk warned about.

**Backups sit in the floor-building gate rather than earlier** because the fix is almost always a change to account structure or a paid feature, and both need the credibility and the budget conversation that Gate B earns you. They sit no later than that because ransomware and destructive extortion do not wait for your quarter to end, and because a company that cannot restore has no floor at all, whatever else is in place.

**Documentation and inventory finish the quarter** because documentation written before the controls exist is fiction that you later have to retract in front of a customer. They also produce the most visible external value, which makes them the right thing to be finishing when the day 90 review lands.

**Why design review is rarely first.** The original talk puts the software development lifecycle and design reviews first in the security engineering column and calls it really big and really important, and that is correct as a statement of long-run value. It is not correct as a statement of week one sequencing, for three reasons. First, you cannot review a design well in an environment you do not understand, and a shallow review in week one costs you credibility that takes a quarter to rebuild. Second, design review is the area most dependent on relationships, and you do not have them yet. Third, and specific to 2026: the volume of code produced per engineer has risen sharply with assisted development, so a human review gate is no longer a viable rate limiter on its own. The durable version of this work is ownership of the paved road (the repository template, the infrastructure module, the reusable pipeline definition), and you cannot own a paved road you have not yet driven. So it usually enters in Gate C as ad hoc, opt-in, inbound reviews, exactly as the original talk describes the fallback when no structured lifecycle exists: spread the word that you do these, recommend it, show value, let word spread, accept incomplete coverage. If the company happens to be shipping a genuinely high-risk feature in your first three weeks, pull it forward for that one feature only. That is a finding creating an exception, which is the correct mechanism.

**Why a paid bug bounty is deferred past 90 days in almost all cases.** The original talk's parenthetical is "hold off if you can," and the speaker's notes are candid that he had not had a truly great experience with bug bounty. The reasoning holds and has strengthened. A bounty program converts your inbound queue into a firehose of low-severity reports you must triage on someone else's schedule, and it does so before you have the ability to fix what comes in. If you cannot yet rotate a credential in an afternoon, you cannot absorb a report that requires a rotation. What you should do inside 90 days is the small, cheap subset: publish a vulnerability disclosure contact and a one paragraph policy saying you will not pursue good faith researchers. That gives you the inbound path without the obligation. Open a paid bounty when three things are true: you can fix a reported issue in under a week, someone other than you can triage, and you have budget for payouts that will not be raided. Detail is in `references/se-4-bug-bounty-and-disclosure.md`. If a founder pushes for a bounty in the first quarter because a competitor has one, say plainly that it is a marketing decision being made as a security decision, and offer the disclosure policy instead.

## Adapting the plan: branches that change everything

Everything above assumes an unremarkable business-to-business software company with fewer than 200 people, in-house engineering, one cloud estate, and no acquisitions. The following facts should visibly change the plan. When one appears, say so explicitly, regenerate `90-DAY-PLAN.md`, and record the change in `DECISION-LOG.md` with the triggering fact.

**A live incident is in progress.** Everything stops. Go to `references/dr-1-incident-response-plan.md` and `references/04-interrupts.md`. Push all current work onto `CONTEXT-STACK.md` as a frame. The plan resumes after, and it will be a better plan, because an incident is the highest quality environment discovery you will ever get. Write down what you learn while it hurts.

**The commitment review surfaced an unmet contractual commitment.** Promote whatever satisfies it to the front of the queue and tell the founders in writing the same day, in `DECISION-LOG.md` and in a message. Unmet contractual security terms are a legal exposure, not a backlog item, and the founders, not you, decide how to handle the gap. Your job is to make sure they know, in writing, with a date.

**A deal is blocked on a certification.** Do not start the audit. Do the sequence: find out what the customer actually requires (often a questionnaire and a security review, not a report), get the deal size and the close date, then propose the cheapest path that unblocks it. Common cheapest paths are a completed questionnaire, a security page, a customer call with you on it, and a written commitment with a date. Note that a written commitment to a customer that a control exists or will exist by a date is a promise the company is making, not one you make on your own authority: it needs a named approver. If a real report is genuinely required and the deal justifies the cost, compress the documentation work into Gate C and accept that workplace security and part of the signal work slip. Say out loud which items are slipping.

**A regulated data class is present.** Health information, payment card data, children's data, biometric data, or data from a regulated sector moves the data inventory from Gate D to Gate A, because the legal obligations attach whether or not you know about them. Find out in week one which categories exist and where they live. The framework choice stops being optional.

**The company sells to consumers rather than to businesses.** Johnson's own slide on what determines priority puts this first, and his speaker notes call it, in his opinion, the biggest single thing that will inform your priorities. Two large changes follow. First, `references/se-5-consumer-account-security.md` enters the plan and enters it early: account takeover, credential stuffing, password reset and account recovery flows, session handling, and the support path that can reset an account are the live attack surface, and unlike a business customer there is no procurement process standing in front of it. The consumer-facing abuse and fraud picture also belongs in Gate A discovery, because it is often already happening and already visible in logs nobody reads. Second, most of the compliance domain drops well down the ordering, because the questionnaires will not arrive. There is no vendor security review from a consumer. Do not build a questionnaire answer bank for an audience that will never send you a questionnaire; mark that cell not applicable with the reason and revisit it the day the company signs its first enterprise pilot. Public security documentation shrinks too, but does not vanish: a vulnerability disclosure contact still matters, because researchers will find the consumer product, and a plain-language privacy and security statement matters to consumers in a way a control matrix does not. Regulatory attention also arrives from a different direction for a consumer product: consumer privacy law, children's data rules if the product is reachable by minors, and payment rules if the product takes cards directly, so the data inventory tends to move earlier than Gate D.

**Engineering is outsourced, offshore, contracted, or was inherited from an agency.** This is the branch where the normal ordering breaks hardest, and it needs `references/09-outsourced-engineering.md` loaded before anything else is planned. Asset ownership recovery jumps ahead of almost every other step, because every other control you might build sits on top of assets the company may not actually own. Before you can secure the cloud account, the company has to own the cloud account. Before you can enforce anything on the source code host, the company has to own the organisation rather than being a guest in the agency's. The concrete Gate A questions become: whose name is on the cloud account, the domain registrar, the source code host organisation, the application store listings, the certificate authority account, the email tenant, and the production database; which of those are billed to a personal card belonging to someone who no longer works on this; who holds the recovery email and phone number for each; and what happens to access on the day the contract ends. Until those answers exist, a control you build can be removed by someone outside the company without notice, and an offboarding checklist covers only the people you can see. Contractor identity is a second front: contractor accounts often sit outside the identity provider entirely, on personal email addresses, with no device management and no offboarding trigger, so the identity picture you built in Gate A is incomplete by construction until you have enumerated them. Expect this branch to consume most of the first month, expect it to be political because the agency relationship is usually somebody's relationship, and expect the recovery of a single account to require a founder to make a phone call you cannot make.

**The company has acquired another company, or is about to close an acquisition.** Treat the acquired entity as a second full grid, not as a migration task. Its identity provider, cloud accounts, source code host, endpoint fleet, backups, and contractual commitments are a complete second estate with its own unknowns, and its security posture is now the acquiring company's posture whether or not the integration has happened. Two rules govern this branch. First, run the whole Gate A discovery again against the acquired estate, with its own state rows, rather than assuming diligence covered it: commercial diligence asks whether controls were claimed, not whether they work. Second, and this is the hard stop: no tenant merge, no directory federation, no trust relationship between the two identity providers, and no shared network or cloud account access happens before `references/dr-0-compromise-assessment.md` has been run against the acquired estate and produced a written result. Merging identity is the act that converts their compromise into your compromise, and it is very hard to reverse. If the acquired estate's log retention has already expired and the assessment cannot see far enough back, that is itself a finding: say so plainly, put it in `RISK-REGISTER.md` with a named accepter, and let the founders decide whether to proceed knowing that. The commitment review also runs twice, because the acquired entity's customer contracts now bind the combined company, and its privacy notices may promise things the acquiring company does not do.

**You are not the first, and the company is larger or past a Series B funding round.** The job is different and the plan should look different. There is inherited state: policies, a control spreadsheet, a previous audit report, perhaps a tool that somebody bought. Treat all of it as untrusted until re-verified. The governing distinction is that the previous program's artifacts are evidence of claims, not evidence of controls. A policy document proves that somebody wrote a policy. A completed control matrix proves that somebody filled in a control matrix. An audit report proves that a sample of evidence satisfied an auditor on a date in the past, for a scope you have not read yet. None of those prove that the control operates today. So Gate A gains a verification pass: for each control the previous program claims, find the artifact that shows it running now (the current administrator list, the current alert firing, the current offboarding ticket for the most recent leaver) and set the state row from what you found rather than from what the document says. Expect two specific failure patterns. The first is a control that was true on audit day and has silently lapsed since, which is extremely common with access reviews and offboarding. The second is scope drift, where the audited scope covers one product or one cloud account and the company has since shipped three more that nobody added. Also expect the political dimension: somebody built the previous program, that somebody may still be at the company, and re-verification will read as an audit of them unless you frame it as re-baselining for you personally. Do it anyway, and be scrupulously factual in how you record the results. `references/08-when-it-is-not-working.md` covers the case where the inherited program is not merely stale but actively misrepresented.

**The company is pre-revenue, or the real reason you were hired is a fundraise or an acquirer's technical due diligence.** Say this out loud, because the default plan is anchored to revenue in ways that will quietly mislead you: deals unblocked, questionnaire turnaround, and contracts already signed are all unavailable as inputs and as measures. What to drop: the questionnaire answer bank entirely until a second real questionnaire actually arrives, and public security documentation reduced to a vulnerability reporting address that someone monitors, plus a `security.txt` file if the product has a website. Do not write a security page describing controls a pre-revenue company does not have. What replaces them is a diligence pack, and the useful property of the diligence pack is that it is assembled from artifacts the normal plan already produces rather than being extra work: the architecture diagram, the data inventory, the access review with administrator counts before and after, the incident response plan, and the risk register with named acceptances. That set is close to what a technical due diligence security section actually asks for. Add to it the things diligence asks for that nothing else in the plan produces: the software licence position for anything in the product, confirmation that the company owns the intellectual property that contractors and founders wrote, the list of subprocessors and where data physically sits, and a straight answer to whether there has ever been a security incident. What kills a term sheet or reprices a deal is rarely a weak control, because acquirers expect weak controls at this stage. What kills it is a surprise: an unreported incident that surfaces in diligence, customer data in a place the data inventory did not mention, intellectual property the company cannot prove it owns, or a contractual commitment nobody disclosed. Your value in this branch is finding the surprises before the acquirer does, and giving the founders time to disclose them on their own terms. Because the standard commercial measures are unavailable, the substitute measures for this situation belong in `references/05-metrics-and-comms.md`.

**The company had a breach or a serious incident before you arrived.** This is common, and the original talk noted that security teams usually start a few months after something really bad happened. There are two very different versions and you must find out which one you are in.

The straightforward version: the incident was properly scoped, handled, and closed, and there is a findings list with unfixed remediation items on it. Two effects follow. You have unusual political capital, so spend it in Gate B on the expensive-to-authorize changes rather than saving it. And the unfixed remediation items are the highest priority work available, because they are already agreed to be problems, which means you skip the argument entirely.

The dangerous version, which is at least as common: the incident was never properly scoped, or was scoped and quietly closed, or a notification obligation was never met or was met late, and you are now the only person in the building who understands that. From the moment you write a conclusion into a durable file, this becomes a live legal exposure for the company and a personal one for you. The incident response reference has a section written for exactly this situation, "An incident that happened before you arrived," in `references/dr-1-incident-response-plan.md`, and you should load it before you do anything else. The rules in brief: reconstruct the timeline from artifacts before you ask any person anything, because questions change what people remember and what they will say later. Do not write a conclusion about whether a breach occurred or whether notification was required into any durable file. Route to counsel first and ask whether the review should run under legal privilege, which is the same pattern the commitment review already uses for contract analysis. The decision to notify late belongs to the chief executive and to counsel, and it never belongs to you. And if the company declines to act on advice you have given in writing, that is a risk acceptance: record it in `RISK-REGISTER.md` with a named accepter and a date, because that record is the thing that protects you.

**The team is tiny, under about 15 people.** Cut the process, keep the controls. Skip written checklists in favor of a single owner who does the thing. Skip the tabletop and do a 30 minute conversation. Do not build a questionnaire answer bank until you have received two questionnaires. Identity, secrets, endpoint, and backups still all apply.

**The team is fully remote.** Workplace security shrinks to hardware logistics and device handling, and endpoint work grows, because the laptop is the office. Add a step in Gate C on what happens when a laptop is lost or an employee is unreachable. Remove any assumption of a corporate network, including network-based monitoring in the signal set, and replace it with identity and endpoint signals.

**The company is already in an audit or has an auditor engaged.** The auditor's control list becomes an input, not a plan. Map their requested controls onto the grid, find the ones that are real security work (access reviews, logging, incident response) and do those in the normal sequence. For the ones that are purely evidentiary, do them cheaply and do not let them expand. Warn the human, once and clearly, that passing an audit and being secure are different achievements and the gap between them is where breached companies live.

**The product is artificial intelligence heavy or ships agents.** Add the model security material from `references/07-modern-cells.md` into Gate A discovery (what models, what data reaches them, what tools can they call, who can change the prompts) and into Gate C design reviews. The practical screening question for any agent feature is whether it simultaneously has access to private data, exposure to untrusted content, and an outbound channel. Any two of those is usually survivable. All three means an attacker who can place text anywhere the agent reads can extract data. If the company also uses internal agents with tool access, treat that tool surface as a supply chain, because it is one.

**Engineering has no continuous integration or no cloud account.** Rare but it happens. Remove the corresponding steps rather than faking them, and write "n/a, verified on <date>" in `SECURITY-STATE.md` so a future reader knows the difference between absent and unexamined.

## What a good day 30, 60, and 90 review looks like

These are the moments where a first security hire either becomes a partner to the founders or becomes a cost center. Prepare all three. Keep them short. Never present a finding without a decision attached.

**Standing rules for all three reviews.** Maximum ten slides or two pages. No raw scanner output. No severity counts without a story. Every risk you present has a named owner and a proposed decision, so the meeting is a decision meeting rather than a status meeting. Anything you want the founders to accept as a risk is stated in one sentence and recorded in `RISK-REGISTER.md` with their name against it after the meeting. Nothing in these reviews cites a gate or a cell identifier as a reason for anything. Every item traces to a fact about this company.

### Day 30 review: "Here is what I found"

1. **What I was hired to do**, in the words of the person who hired you, from your Gate A conversation. This closes the expectation gap in public.
2. **What exists today**, one slide: the architecture diagram with data stores marked.
3. **The five things that would hurt us most**, ranked, one line each, plain language, no jargon. Say what happens to the business, not what the vulnerability is called.
4. **What I already fixed**, with evidence. Usually the identity and secrets work. Keep this to three items and make them concrete.
5. **What I need a decision on**, at most three items, each with a recommendation and a cost.
6. **Commitments I found in contracts**, if the commitment review turned up anything the founders did not know about. This slide is often the most valuable thirty seconds of your first month.
7. **What I am doing next**, four bullets, the Gate C objective.

If the compromise assessment found indicators, that is not a slide in this review. That is a conversation you had on the day you found it.

### Day 60 review: "Here is the floor"

1. **Progress against the five things from day 30**, same order, same wording, with status. Reusing the exact wording is deliberate: it shows you did not move the goalposts.
2. **The controls that now run without me**, listing onboarding and offboarding, endpoint enrollment, alerting, with the named owner of each.
3. **The incident response plan on one slide**: who declares, who leads, where we coordinate, who tells customers, and the contractual clock. Ask the founders to confirm they agree they are the ones who decide on customer notification.
4. **Can we come back from a bad day**, in one line: what is backed up, whether a restore has been performed and how long it took, and whether the same person who can delete production can delete the backups.
5. **What it costs**, honestly: current tool spend, current logging spend, and the trend.
6. **Where we are still blind**, said plainly. This slide builds more trust than any other, and it is where you pre-empt the "why didn't you catch this" conversation you may need later.
7. **The one decision I need this month**, usually endpoint tooling budget or framework choice.
8. **What I am doing next**, the Gate D objective.

### Day 90 review: "Here is the program, and here is next quarter"

1. **The grid on one slide**, all rows, with status colouring from `SECURITY-STATE.md`, including the rows marked not applicable with their reasons. This is the single best artifact you will produce in the quarter because it makes an abstract job legible in five seconds. Present it as coverage of your own blind spots, not as a score.
2. **What changed in 90 days**, five lines, before and after, with numbers. Administrators went from eleven to three. Long-lived cloud keys in the pipeline went from six to zero. Departed employees with active access went from four to zero. Time to restore the production database went from unknown to fifty minutes.
3. **What it enabled commercially**: questionnaires completed and by whom, deals unblocked, the public security page. For a pre-revenue or consumer company, substitute the measures from `references/05-metrics-and-comms.md`, because these three are unavailable and pretending otherwise reads as padding.
4. **Residual risk**, the top five from `RISK-REGISTER.md`, each with owner and decision, including the accepted ones with the accepting name shown.
5. **Framework recommendation** with cost, timeline, and the explicit option of waiting, plus what would change the recommendation.
6. **Next quarter in four objectives**, one per gate-equivalent, no step list.
7. **What I need**: budget, headcount, or authority, stated as a specific number and a specific decision, not as a general appeal. Guidance on framing this is in `references/05-metrics-and-comms.md`.

## Deliberately not in the first 90 days

State these out loud when the human or a founder proposes them, along with the reason and the condition that would change your mind. Being able to say what you are not doing, and why, is most of what separates a program from a backlog.

- **A paid bug bounty program.** Reason: it creates inbound work you cannot yet absorb and pays for reports you cannot yet fix. Instead: publish a disclosure contact and safe harbour statement in Gate D. Revisit when triage is not solely yours.
- **A SOC 2 or ISO 27001 audit engagement.** Reason: auditing controls that do not exist yet produces a document rather than security, plus recurring cost. Instead: do the underlying controls, make the framework decision, and start the audit in the following quarter. Exception: a deal large enough to justify it, with the founders explicitly accepting the tradeoff in `DECISION-LOG.md`.
- **A security information and event management platform purchase.** Reason: expensive, requires an operator, and useless without a consumption model and a chosen signal set. Instead: get logs into whatever queryable store already exists and alert on a small number of things.
- **Penetration testing.** Reason: a test in month two finds issues you already know about and cannot yet fix, and burns budget. It is also an active test that requires written authorisation and a defined scope before anyone touches anything. Instead: schedule it for the quarter after the floor exists, when the findings will be new information. Exception: a customer contractually requires a report, which the commitment review will have told you.
- **A formal risk assessment methodology or a control framework spreadsheet with hundreds of rows.** Reason: it is enterprise machinery for an organisation that has neither the population nor the process to feed it. Instead: `RISK-REGISTER.md` with real risks, real owners, and real decisions.
- **Rewriting authentication or authorization in the product.** Reason: it is a multi-quarter engineering project, it is not yours to own alone, and proposing it in month one marks you as someone who does not understand delivery pressure. Instead: document the risk, get it on the engineering roadmap through the design review relationship you are building.
- **Mandatory security training with a vendor platform.** Reason: low effect, real cost, and it consumes goodwill on something employees find insulting when the basics are not yet in place. Instead: one short, specific, well-written message about the two threats that actually target your company. Revisit when a customer or framework requires it.
- **A written policy set (twelve documents, acceptable use, and so on).** Reason: policies describe controls, and writing them before the controls exist produces documents that are already false. Instead: write policies in the quarter you start the framework work, and generate them from what is actually true.
- **Data loss prevention tooling, a cloud access security broker, or a software-as-a-service security posture platform.** Reason: cost and operational burden out of proportion to a startup's team size. Instead: the free versions of the same outcomes, which are the third-party application approval settings, the corporate card statement reviewed monthly, and a grant register.
- **Filling in a grid row that no finding points at.** Reason: this is the framework generating work, which is the failure this whole file is written to prevent. Instead: mark it n/a with the reason, or leave it unknown and say so at the day 90 review.

Record each of these as a deliberate deferral in `DECISION-LOG.md` the first time it comes up, with the date and the condition that would reopen it. When a founder asks in month five why you have not done a penetration test, the answer should be a link to a dated decision rather than a memory.

## Wiring the plan to state

Every gate transition updates state. Do this without being asked.

- On entering a gate: write the gate name, its objective, and its exit criteria into `90-DAY-PLAN.md` under a heading for that gate, with each exit criterion as an unchecked item. Write only the steps that findings have actually justified, each with its identifier and the finding that justified it. A step with no finding attached does not go in the file.
- On completing a step: update the relevant row in `SECURITY-STATE.md` with the new status and the evidence line. Never change a status to done without an evidence line. Never change a status to n/a without a written reason.
- On any decision, including a no-go: append to `DECISION-LOG.md` with the date, the decision, the reasoning, and the approving human's name.
- On any risk you are not fixing now, and on any item you are deferring: append to `RISK-REGISTER.md` with severity, owner, decision, accepted-by, status of open, and a review date. An empty accepted-by field means nobody has accepted it and you must chase that. The risk register is where deferred work lives; there is no separate backlog file.
- On any access you request: append to `ACCESS-LOG.md` with what, from whom, when, and the outcome. Pending requests older than five days get raised in the next session.
- On any interrupt: push and pop frames on `CONTEXT-STACK.md` per `references/04-interrupts.md`, and note in `90-DAY-PLAN.md` which step identifier was parked.
- On exiting a gate: check the exit criteria in `90-DAY-PLAN.md`, and for any criterion not met, either move it forward explicitly with a reason or open a risk for it. Do not silently drop an exit criterion.

Before proposing the first step of any gate, re-read `SECURITY-STATE.md` and `RISK-REGISTER.md`. The plan is regenerated from facts, not recited from this file. If you ever find yourself proposing a step and the honest justification is "because it comes next here," you have stopped doing the job, and the correct recovery is to go back to the findings and start again.
