# What the 2019 grid misses in 2026

> **Load when:** you need the *why*. Specifically: the human asks whether the playbook is still current, asks "why isn't cloud on this grid", pushes back on a recommendation because it is not in the original sixteen cells, asks why the shape of a 2019 cell changed, or you are about to build the 90 day plan and need to know which 2019 cells to trust as written and which to update.
>
> **This file is the argument, not the procedure.** It explains what changed between 2019 and 2026 and why, in language you can say out loud to a founder or a chief technology officer. It does not contain the work. When you have decided to actually do something about supply chain, the build pipeline, cloud posture, third-party access grants, artificial intelligence and large language model security, or backups, load `references/07-modern-cells.md`, which owns the procedures, the commands, and the acceptance criteria for cells M-1 through M-6. If you find yourself executing steps out of this file, you loaded the wrong one.

Two clarifications on terms used below, expanded once here: continuous integration and continuous delivery (CI/CD) means the automated system that builds, tests, and ships your code, and the Model Context Protocol (MCP) is an open standard for connecting language models to tools and data sources.

## How to use this file

The grid comes from Evan Johnson's talk "Starting Security at a Startup" at OWASP AppSec California, January 2019. He was the first security engineer at Cloudflare and before that the first security hire at Segment. The grid is sixteen cells across four domains, and it is still the best single-page starting structure for a founding security hire that anyone has published.

This file is the honest delta. It tells you which parts of the 2019 advice to run as written, which parts to update, and which parts are missing entirely. It is not a replacement for the grid. The grid's structure is the skeleton. This file adds the parts of 2026 that the skeleton has no bone for.

Three rules for how you, the agent, use this:

1. **Do not lead with the delta.** When a human is on day one, they need the grid, not a critique of the grid. Bring these themes in when they attach to work already in flight.
2. **Never present a modern theme as "the 2019 talk was wrong."** It was right for 2019. Say "the shape of this cell changed" and explain what changed.
3. **Every theme below names the grid cell it extends.** When you act on a theme, record the status under that cell in `SECURITY-STATE.md`, not in a new freeform section. The grid stays canonical. New cells live in `references/07-modern-cells.md` and are recorded under their own headings there.

## The three structural errors, stated plainly

Before the theme by theme, here is the summary you can give a human in thirty seconds if they ask "so is this thing still good?"

**Error one: Corporate Security is no longer the back office.** In 2019, "Corporate Security" meant laptops, badges, and identity provider hygiene, and it sat fourth in the reading order and last in status. In 2026 it is the primary breach path for a startup. The chain is: information stealing malware lands on an employee laptop or personal device, steals a browser session cookie, the attacker replays that cookie into a software-as-a-service application without ever touching the password or the second factor, and from there reaches source code, the cloud console, or the customer data warehouse. Treat this domain as first among equals, not fourth. Evan himself argued for this in the talk's speaker notes: "Production takes years to fix and corp is something you can do in a few quarters." He was right and the threat landscape has since made it urgent as well as tractable.

**Error two: there is no supply chain domain.** SE-2 says "understanding your tech stack by engineering" and in 2019 that meant your code and your architecture. In 2026 the majority of the executing code in your build and on your developers' machines is code nobody at your company wrote, running with credentials your company issued. That is not a sub-bullet of a cell. It is a fifth column. See `references/07-modern-cells.md`.

**Error three: Compliance is framed as telling people things, not as knowing things.** CO-1 (public docs), CO-2 (questionnaire knowledge base), and CO-3 (existing commitments) are all outbound communication. None of them is "know what data you hold and where it lives." That is the answer to the blank CO-4 cell, and it is the cell that makes the other three honest. Note that Evan filled the blank cell later in the same deck with "GDPR and current laws," which is the 2019 shape of the same idea.

There is also an implicit assumption in the grid that no longer holds: that a security hire's leverage comes from reviewing things. In 2026, with artificial intelligence assisted development raising code volume per engineer several fold, a single human reviewer is not a rate limiter you can stand at. Leverage comes from owning the paved road: the repository template, the infrastructure-as-code module, the reusable continuous integration workflow, the identity provider policy. Review what the paved road cannot cover.

## Theme 1: Software supply chain and malicious dependencies

**What 2019 assumed.** The dependency risk model was Equifax and Apache Struts: an old library with a published vulnerability that you failed to patch. The fix was a scanner and a patch cadence. The threat was passive, the timeline was months, and "scan before production" was a sensible control point.

**What actually changed.** The threat became active. The dominant risk is now a current, up-to-date, popular package whose latest version is deliberately malicious, published either by a compromised maintainer account or by a worm. The reference case is the self-replicating npm campaign that began in September 2025 and returned in later waves: it stole developer and continuous integration credentials, then used those stolen publishing tokens to poison other packages the victim maintained, spreading without human involvement, and exfiltrated through a workflow file it dropped into the victim's own repositories. Later waves affected hundreds of packages with billions of monthly downloads. Delivery is usually a package install script, and the machine that gets compromised is a developer laptop or a build runner, not production.

**Why this matters to you specifically.** Two consequences a beginner usually misses.

First, your time to compromise is *installation*, not deployment. A control that runs before production deploy is architecturally too late. Software composition analysis tools that alert on published vulnerability identifiers will not fire at all, because for the first several hours a malicious version has no identifier assigned to it. You are not detecting a known-bad list, you are constraining behaviour.

Second, the credential you lose is not a database password. It is a package registry token or a source control personal access token. That means you are no longer the victim of a supply chain incident, you are the *source* of one, with disclosure obligations to your own customers. That reframing is what gets you engineering time.

**Grid attachment.** Extends SE-2 (understanding your tech stack) and SE-3 (secrets and keys, because the loss is a credential loss). It is large enough that it gets its own modern cell, M-1, in `references/07-modern-cells.md`.

**Concrete first action.** Ask one question and get one number: "How many days old is the youngest dependency version we would install today, and is there anything stopping a brand new version from being installed on a laptop right now?" Then propose the single highest-yield control, which is a version cooldown: block package versions younger than seven to fourteen days unless a human explicitly waives it. Most published-malware campaigns are detected and the packages removed within hours to days, so a cooldown neutralises the majority of them with no scanner, no vendor, and no per-package triage. It is available natively in some package managers and registries, and can be enforced by a private registry proxy or a continuous integration check otherwise. Record it under SE-2 in `SECURITY-STATE.md` with the evidence being the actual configuration file or proxy setting, not someone's assurance.

**The other four controls, in order of value after the cooldown.**

1. A single registry chokepoint. One private proxy (options across ecosystems include Artifactory, Verdaccio, GitHub Packages, GitLab Package Registry, AWS CodeArtifact, Google Artifact Registry, Azure Artifacts) as the only route to the public registry from laptops *and* from build runners. Without this you have no kill switch and no way to answer "who pulled it and when."
2. Disable install scripts by default, with an explicit allowlist. In the npm ecosystem this is the `ignore-scripts` setting; other ecosystems have equivalents or do not run arbitrary code at install at all. Most repositories need fewer than twenty exceptions.
3. Publishing provenance. If you publish packages, turn on trusted publishing and provenance attestation so consumers can verify your releases came from your pipeline.
4. A software bill of materials whose only job is answering one question in under thirty minutes: "are we running package X at version Y anywhere, including inside build images?" Generate it in the build, store it somewhere queryable. If it lives in a compliance folder and nobody has ever queried it, you built the wrong artifact.

Write the runbook too. The first step of a malicious package runbook is not "remove the package." It is "rotate every credential a developer laptop or a build runner could have reached in the last thirty days."

## Theme 2: The build pipeline is the crown jewel

**What 2019 assumed.** The crown jewel was production. Continuous integration was plumbing, and the security question about it was whether tests ran.

**What actually changed.** The build pipeline is now the highest value target in most startups, because it has write access to production, holds every secret, executes arbitrary third-party code by design, and has no human sitting at a keyboard to notice something odd. In March 2025 a mutable version tag on a build step used by tens of thousands of repositories was repointed at a payload that dumped the runner's memory into world-readable build logs, and that compromise was itself enabled by a prior build-step compromise that leaked a token (`references/07-modern-cells.md`, M-2, owns the full account and the controls). Step-to-step lateral movement inside build pipelines is a demonstrated technique, not a theoretical one.

**Why this matters to you specifically.** Do this exercise in your first two weeks: list every identity in the company ordered by privilege. The build system will be at or near the top, above every human. It typically has cloud deployment rights, package publishing rights, repository write access, and secret access, with no multi-factor authentication, no session time limit, and an audit trail nobody has ever opened. And it is reachable by an untrusted external contributor through pull request triggers that run with elevated permissions, and by any third-party action or plugin author.

**Grid attachment.** Nothing in the 2019 grid covers this. New cell M-2 in `references/07-modern-cells.md`. Its credential half also feeds SE-3, and its detection half feeds DR-2.

**Concrete first action.** Kill long-lived cloud keys in the build system and replace them with short-lived federated credentials. This is the single highest-value control in this entire file and it usually takes about a day of engineering time.

The mechanism is the same everywhere and the names differ. The build platform issues a signed short-lived token that describes the job. The cloud provider is configured to trust that issuer and to exchange the token for a session, but only when the token's claims match a specific repository and a specific branch or environment.

- GitHub Actions to AWS: configure an OpenID Connect identity provider in AWS Identity and Access Management, then an assumable role whose trust policy conditions on the `sub` claim, for example `repo:acme/api:ref:refs/heads/main` or `repo:acme/api:environment:production`.
- GitHub Actions to Google Cloud: Workload Identity Federation, with an attribute condition on the repository and ref, bound to a service account.
- GitHub Actions to Azure: a federated credential on an app registration or user-assigned managed identity, with subject matching the same repository and ref pattern.
- GitLab CI to any of the three: the equivalent using GitLab's ID token, conditioning on `project_path` and `ref`.
- If they use CircleCI, Buildkite, Jenkins, or something else, ask which of these the platform supports before promising it. Some support OpenID Connect, some do not.

The condition on branch or environment is the part beginners skip and it is the part that matters. Without it, a pull request from a fork or a throwaway feature branch can assume the deploy role.

**The other controls, in order.**

1. Pin every third-party action or plugin to a full commit hash, not a tag, and enforce it with a policy check in the build. Tags are mutable. That is the entire story of the March 2025 incident.
2. Split the build identity from the deploy identity. The build job runs untrusted code (dependencies, tests, contributor pull requests). The deploy job runs no untrusted code and holds the privileged credential. They must not be the same job with the same token.
3. Ban elevated-permission pull request triggers and self-hosted runners on public repositories unless a human has reviewed the specific case. Poisoned pipeline execution is almost always one of those two.
4. Egress control and file integrity monitoring on runners, either a hardening action or ephemeral self-hosted runners with a network policy. If a build step suddenly connects to a network you have never used, that should reach a person.
5. Branch protection that administrators cannot bypass, required reviews, and an alert on any change to the protection ruleset or to workflow definition files inside a pull request. Treat workflow files as production infrastructure code, because that is exactly what they are.
6. A quarterly "who can push to production" report derived from actual permissions, not from the organisation chart.

## Theme 3: Cloud posture and identity replaced the network perimeter

**What 2019 assumed.** The grid has no cloud cell at all. In 2019 that was already questionable. In 2026 it is disqualifying. The implicit model was that production is a set of servers behind a network boundary and that securing it means securing the boundary and the code inside it.

**What actually changed.** The perimeter is an access policy document, not a network. A startup in 2026 ships managed services, serverless functions, and managed data stores across several accounts or projects or subscriptions. The recurring catastrophic failure is not an exploited service. It is a misconfiguration: a publicly readable object storage bucket, a role with a wildcard permission on a data store, a long-lived access key committed to a repository, a database with a public endpoint. No exploit chain required, and the outcome is a full breach notification event.

**Why this matters to you specifically.** This is where a solo hire produces the most risk reduction per hour, because the failure modes are enumerable by a free tool and the fixes are code changes that engineers already know how to make. It is also the domain where "I did not know that was public" ends careers.

**Grid attachment.** New cell M-3 in `references/07-modern-cells.md`. It borrows from SE-2 (you cannot secure an architecture you cannot draw) and CS-1 (workload identity is identity).

**Concrete first action.** Run a read-only posture baseline and read the diff, not the score.

- Free and vendor neutral: Prowler or ScoutSuite, both of which run against AWS, Google Cloud, and Azure with read-only credentials. Run with a read-only role. Example, read-only: `prowler aws --severity critical high`. Confirm the exact subcommand with `prowler --help` before running, since the command surface changes between major versions.
- Native and low cost: AWS Security Hub with its foundational standard, Google Security Command Center at the standard tier, Microsoft Defender for Cloud's free tier. All three give you a baseline without a procurement conversation.
- Paid, later, when funded: the cloud-native application protection platform category (Wiz, Orca, and similar) generally starts in the tens of thousands per year. Do not start here. Start with the free scan and the organisation-level guardrails, because the paid tool will just find the same first hundred things.

Then move from per-resource fixes to organisation-level guardrails, which is what actually stops the problem recurring: service control policies in AWS Organizations, organization policy constraints in Google Cloud, Azure Policy with deny effects. The high value set is the same everywhere: block public object storage, deny disabling of audit logging, deny use of the root or owner account for daily work, deny creation of long-lived user access keys, restrict to the regions you actually use.

Two more things that pay for themselves. First, zero long-lived static keys: single sign-on for humans, attached roles or managed identities for workloads, federated tokens for the build system. If a long-lived key identifier exists anywhere in the account, that is an open finding in `RISK-REGISTER.md`. Second, a one-page architecture diagram with every data store marked and, next to each, who can read it. If you cannot draw it you cannot secure it, and every questionnaire and every audit will ask for it anyway, so you are building an artifact you need twice.

## Theme 4: Software-as-a-service sprawl and third-party access grants

**What 2019 assumed.** Vendor risk meant a spreadsheet of vendors and their contracts. The security boundary was still around your own systems.

**What actually changed.** Your crown jewel data now lives in other people's systems, and the way attackers reach it is by compromising an integration rather than you. Stolen authorisation refresh tokens for one widely installed integration reached more than seven hundred organisations in 2025 (`references/07-modern-cells.md`, M-4, owns the full account and the controls). Nobody's password was cracked, no second factor was defeated, and no laptop was touched. The only decision any victim made was clicking Authorize on an integration, sometimes years earlier, often by an employee with no administrative role at all.

**Why this matters to you specifically.** Third-party grants into your business tools are long-lived, invisible to endpoint tooling, not covered by your single sign-on policy, and installable by any employee unless an administrator turned that off. Refresh tokens survive password rotation and survive multi-factor authentication enforcement. Revocation is the only response, and most teams do not know where the revoke button is until they are already in an incident.

**Grid attachment.** New cell M-4 in `references/07-modern-cells.md`. Touches CS-1 (identity and access) and CO-4 (you cannot register grants without a data inventory).

**Concrete first action.** Restrict third-party application installation to administrator approval in every platform in use. This is roughly a twenty minute change per platform and it is default-open in most of them.

- Google Workspace: Admin console, Security, then Access and data control, then API controls. Set third-party app access to allow only trusted apps, and configure the internal app allowlist.
- Microsoft 365 and Entra ID: Enterprise applications, then Consent and permissions. Turn off user consent for applications and enable the admin consent request workflow so people can still ask.
- Slack: workspace or organisation settings, Apps, then app management settings. Require administrator approval for app installation.
- GitHub organisation: Settings, then Third-party Access and OAuth app policy. Require approval, and separately review installed GitHub Apps.
- GitLab: group and instance level settings restrict authorised applications and integrations.
- If you do not yet know which platforms exist, that is a recon question, not a control question. Go to `references/01-recon.md`.

Then build an authorisation grant register: application name, scopes granted, who approved it, business owner, what data it can reach, date granted. Review quarterly and revoke anything nobody claims. Store it as a table under CS-1 in `SECURITY-STATE.md` until it outgrows that, then give it its own file.

Also write a revocation runbook per platform, with the exact administrative page, and test it once on a harmless integration so you are not discovering the interface during an incident.

Two cheap discovery techniques that beat buying a tool at this stage: read the corporate card statement monthly, and read the third-party access audit log in your identity provider monthly. Software-as-a-service posture management tools are worth money later. Those two are worth more now, and they cost nothing.

One purchasing rule worth writing down early: no single sign-on, no customer data. Putting that sentence in the vendor policy is more valuable at seed stage than any tool you could buy.

## Theme 5: Artificial intelligence and large language model security

**What 2019 assumed.** Nothing. This did not exist as a startup concern.

**What actually changed.** Three separate surfaces appeared, and people constantly conflate them. Separate them out loud every time, because the controls are completely different.

**Surface one: your product calls a model.** Prompt injection is the core issue and it is best understood as an unsolved input-trust problem rather than a bug awaiting a patch. The decision rule to use is the lethal trifecta test (`references/07-modern-cells.md`, M-5, owns the test, the wording, and the worked examples). It is architectural: the fix is to break one leg of the trifecta, not to filter the prompt, because filtering is a speed bump rather than a control. Most deployed agent features fail the test, usually without anyone having noticed.

**Surface two: your company runs agents internally.** The Model Context Protocol, an open standard for connecting models to tools and data, made the internal tool surface explode. Scanning in 2025 and 2026 repeatedly found large numbers of internet-exposed protocol servers, a substantial share of them with no authentication at all. The documented attack classes are worth naming because they are not intuitive: tool poisoning, where a tool's description contains instructions aimed at the model; rug pulls, where a server silently redefines a tool after the user approved it; tool shadowing, where one server's tool description manipulates how another server's tool is used; and confused deputy across servers. There has been at least one case of an integration server shipping many clean releases to build trust before adding a single line that copied messages to an attacker address. This is package supply chain risk with a shorter fuse and a much less mature review culture.

**Surface three: your engineers write code with assistants.** More code per engineer, code that looks plausible and reviews easily, more secrets committed because generation is fast, and more dependencies added without any human ever having considered who maintains them. This is the surface that quietly amplifies Themes 1 and 2.

**Why this matters to you specifically.** Someone will ask you to approve an agent feature in your first quarter and you need a decision rule, not a vibe. Use the trifecta test as the rule. Also, agent tool servers on developer machines are unlogged, unauthenticated, and often reachable, and nobody is treating them as production, so nobody is watching.

**Grid attachment.** New cell M-5 in `references/07-modern-cells.md`. Surface one attaches to SE-1 (design review). Surface two attaches to CS-2 (endpoint) and M-1 (supply chain). Surface three attaches to SE-3 (secrets) and SE-1.

**Concrete first action.** Take an inventory of the three surfaces separately, in one page, and write it under SE-1 in `SECURITY-STATE.md`. Then apply the trifecta test to whatever agent feature is closest to shipping, and write the result in `DECISION-LOG.md` with a date and the approver. Do not write an "AI policy" first. A policy with no inventory behind it is theatre and engineers will read it as such.

## Theme 6: Phishing-resistant authentication replaced 2019-era multi-factor

**What 2019 assumed.** Multi-factor authentication was a binary. Turning it on was the win. One-time codes from an authenticator app were the good answer and short message service codes were the acceptable answer.

**What actually changed.** Adversary-in-the-middle phishing kits made all shared-secret second factors, including authenticator app codes and push approvals, routinely bypassable. The kit proxies the real login page, the victim enters the real code, the kit relays it and captures the resulting session. Push fatigue attacks defeat approval prompts by simply sending many of them. The only categorically different answer is an authenticator bound to the site's origin: hardware security keys or platform passkeys built on the FIDO2 and WebAuthn standards. Origin binding means the credential simply will not produce a signature for the attacker's domain, so the phishing page gets nothing worth relaying.

**Why this matters to you specifically.** "We have multi-factor authentication" is now an answer that means very little, and you will hear it as reassurance in your first week. Ask the follow-up: which factor types are actually permitted, and is there a fallback path that downgrades to a code. The fallback is almost always where the attack lands.

**Grid attachment.** CS-1 (identity and access management), same cell, updated bar. This is a case where the 2019 cell is exactly right and only the acceptance criterion moved.

**Concrete first action.** Do not start with a company-wide rollout. Start with the small set of accounts that would end the company: identity provider administrators, cloud administrators, source control administrators, finance and payroll, and the domain registrar. Require phishing-resistant factors for that group, and crucially remove the weaker fallback for those accounts, because leaving the fallback in place means you changed nothing. Then expand.

Platform notes without assuming a vendor: Google Workspace has a two-step verification policy that can be set to security key only for an organisational unit. Microsoft Entra ID does this with authentication strength in Conditional Access, choosing the phishing-resistant strength. Okta calls it authenticator assurance in an authentication policy. If they use something else, ask "can we require a phishing-resistant factor for one group only, and can we remove the weaker fallback for that group" before promising a timeline. Every account in that top group needs two enrolled keys, because one lost key with no backup is a lockout, and a lockout during a hiring freeze is the kind of self-inflicted incident that costs you credibility. Confirm the backup enrollment before you enforce anything, and treat enforcement as a mutating change requiring an explicit human yes per the behavioural contract.

## Theme 7: Information-stealing malware and session token theft

**What 2019 assumed.** Endpoint security meant antivirus and disk encryption, and the cell carried a marker in the original deck indicating it was the cheap, table-stakes win. The implicit threat was ransomware or a commodity trojan.

**What actually changed.** The dominant commodity threat against a startup is now malware whose entire purpose is to copy browser cookies, saved credentials, and session tokens, and sell them within hours on a market. Delivery is usually social: a fake software download, a malicious search advertisement, a job applicant attachment, an installer for a tool the employee genuinely wanted, or an instruction pasted from a web page telling the user to run a command to fix a supposed error. Once the session cookie is out, the attacker signs in as the employee with no password and no second factor, because the session is already authenticated. Two consequences: the compromise can be on a personal or unmanaged device that never appears in any inventory, and password resets and multi-factor enrollment do not fix it. Only session revocation does.

**Why this matters to you specifically.** This ties Themes 4 and 6 together into one chain: laptop, to session cookie, to business application, to cloud or source control. It is the single most common path from nothing to a real incident at a company your size, and every link in it is something a solo hire can affect.

**Grid attachment.** CS-2 (endpoint security), same cell, updated threat model. Detection attaches to DR-2 (top security signals), and the response step attaches to DR-1 (incident response plan).

**Concrete first action.** Find out whether you can revoke a session, and how long it takes, before you need to. Write the exact steps in the incident response plan under DR-1.

- Google Workspace: Admin console, the user, then Security, then sign out from all sessions, plus reset any application-specific passwords and review connected applications.
- Microsoft 365 and Entra ID: revoke sessions on the user object, which invalidates refresh tokens, and confirm the sign-in frequency setting in Conditional Access so a revoked session cannot simply persist.
- Okta: clear user sessions from the administrative console or the applicable interface.
- Then separately: source control (revoke personal access tokens and active sessions), chat, and the data warehouse. Each is separate. Revoking the identity provider session does not always revoke the application session, and that gap is exactly what people discover at the worst moment.

Second action: make sure browsers actually update, since the browser is now the security boundary for most of this. Enforce browser updates and restrict extension installation to an allowlist. That is available through Chrome Enterprise policy, Edge policy, and Firefox enterprise policy, all free, and deployable through whatever device management you have. If you have no device management at all, that is the finding to write down, and CS-2 in `references/cs-2-endpoint-security.md` covers the cheapest path from zero.

## Theme 8: Compliance automation changed the sequencing decision

**What 2019 assumed.** Achieving a formal audit certification was a large, expensive, months-long project involving consultants, and the sensible advice was to resist committing to one before you were ready. Evan's note was blunt: the business commits to things like a Type 2 report in three months before anyone has the controls, and that is a bad position to be in.

**What actually changed.** Compliance automation platforms turned much of the evidence collection into an integration problem. Continuous control monitoring agents connect to the cloud provider, the identity provider, the source control system, and the device management tool, and produce evidence automatically. The realistic cost for an early stage startup is roughly ten to thirty thousand dollars per year for the platform plus a similar range for the audit firm, and a first report is achievable in a few months rather than a year. The market includes Vanta, Drata, Secureframe, Sprinto, Thoropass and others, plus a free open source option in Comply-style control libraries if you want to run the program manually. Note that automation collects evidence, it does not create controls. A platform pointed at an environment with no controls produces a very organised list of things that are failing.

**Why this changes the decision, in both directions.** It is now genuinely cheaper and faster to say yes, which means the old advice of "hold off" is weaker. But the platform's connectors also make it very tempting to start compliance before you have any of the underlying controls, which converts your first quarter into evidence chasing. Both failure modes are real.

**The decision rule to give the human.** Start the audit process when, and only when, at least one of these is true:

1. There is a named, quantified deal currently blocked on it, with a person's name and a number attached. Not "enterprise customers will want it eventually."
2. The company already committed to a date publicly or contractually, in which case go to CO-3 and find out exactly what was promised before anything else.
3. Your market simply does not open without it, which is common in health, financial, and government-adjacent segments.

If none is true, spend the quarter on the controls instead. The controls are the expensive part and they are prerequisites either way. Every hour spent on identity, logging, and access review is an hour you do not spend again when the audit does start. Buying the automation platform first, before the controls exist, is the single most common way a first security hire loses their first quarter.

**Grid attachment.** CO-3 (understand existing commitments) drives the decision. CO-4 (data inventory and framework choice) is where you record it. Evan's original advice about not doing questionnaires by hand holds completely and is CO-2.

**Concrete first action.** Before any tooling conversation, answer CO-3: search the contracts, the sales collateral, the website, and the shared drive for every commitment already made, and get the exact wording and dates. Record findings under CO-3 in `SECURITY-STATE.md` and any dated promise as a risk in `RISK-REGISTER.md` with an owner. See `references/co-3-existing-commitments.md`.

## Theme 9: Remote-first changed what workplace security means

**What 2019 assumed.** CS-4, workplace security, meant a physical office: badge access, visitor policy, tailgating, camera coverage, clean desk, network jacks in conference rooms, and monitoring the office network's name resolution traffic, which Evan named as one of his four highest value starting signals.

**What actually changed.** Many startups have no office, or have an office nobody works from, or have an office that is a coworking space where you control nothing and cannot install anything on the network. Meanwhile the things CS-4 was protecting moved: the meeting is a video call anyone with a link can join, the whiteboard is a cloud document, the printer is somebody's home printer, the guest network is a home router shared with a partner's work laptop and a smart television, and the shipping and receiving problem is now onboarding hardware to homes in a dozen countries and getting it back when someone leaves.

**Why this matters to you specifically.** Do not run the 2019 physical checklist against a company that has no premises. It burns your credibility with people who can see it is irrelevant, and it wastes the quarter. But do not skip the cell either, because the underlying risks reappeared in new forms and some have got worse.

**The remote-first translation of CS-4.**

- Video conferencing exposure: default meeting settings, whether links are guessable, whether recordings and transcripts land in a shared location that is broadly readable, and whether external participants can join before a host.
- Shared document defaults: whether the default sharing setting in the document platform is "anyone with the link" and whether public link sharing is even permitted for the drives holding customer data.
- Home network and device separation: what you can actually ask for is a company-managed device, a full disk encrypted machine, and a rule that customer data does not touch a personal device. Auditing home networks is not something you can do and pretending otherwise is theatre.
- Hardware logistics: procurement, shipment, return on departure, and secure disposal, which is a real gap and is genuinely CS-3's problem as much as CS-4's.
- Remote hiring fraud: verifying that the person who interviewed is the person who started, and that they are where they claim to be. This is a live problem in 2026 with documented cases of fraudulent remote workers placed at technology companies, and for a fully remote company that hires engineers who receive production access it is a high-consequence one. It is a joiner-side control, so the procedure lives with onboarding in `references/cs-3-onboarding-offboarding.md` rather than here. Two things to know before you go there. First, this is a people operations, legal, and potentially sanctions matter, not a security incident you handle alone. Second, if you suspect it, do not confront the person and do not unilaterally revoke their access: escalate the same day to the chief executive, to counsel, and to whoever runs people operations, and let them decide the sequence. Acting alone here can destroy evidence, expose the company to an employment claim, and tip off an operation that law enforcement may want to observe.
- The signal that replaced office name resolution monitoring: your identity provider's sign-in logs and your endpoint tooling's telemetry. If there is an office, network-level monitoring is still worth having and is still cheap. If there is not, do not go looking for a network to monitor.

**Grid attachment.** CS-4, same cell, translated. Hardware logistics also attach to CS-3. The replacement signal attaches to DR-2.

**Concrete first action.** Ask three closed questions: is there a physical office that people actually work from, does anyone other than employees have access to it, and are company devices shipped to homes. Those three answers determine whether CS-4 is a physical checklist, a logistics checklist, or both. Record it under CS-4 in `SECURITY-STATE.md` and move on. This is a cheap cell. Do not let it eat a week.

## Theme 10: Backups and recovery, which were never on the grid at all

**What 2019 assumed.** Be honest about this one, because it is a different kind of gap from the other nine. Backups are not a 2019 cell that aged badly. They were simply not on the slide. There is no cell in the original sixteen that says "can you get your data back," and the talk does not cover it. So this theme is an addition to the playbook rather than a modernisation of it, and you should say exactly that if a human asks why you are recommending something with no cell behind it. The reason it was reasonable to leave out in 2019 is that backups were widely understood as an infrastructure reliability concern that engineering already owned, and in many startups that is still where the ownership sits. The reason it cannot be left out now is that it stopped being only a reliability control and became a security control.

**What actually changed.** Three things. First, extortion. The dominant financially motivated attack against a company your size assumes that destroying or encrypting your ability to recover is what converts an intrusion into a payment, and attackers now specifically target backup systems, backup credentials, and snapshot deletion before they do anything visible. A backup an administrator credential can delete is not a backup, it is a copy. Second, the data moved. A meaningful share of the data that would end the company if it vanished now lives in software-as-a-service applications where the vendor's own retention window, often thirty days or less, is the entire recovery story unless somebody chose otherwise. Most teams have never checked what that window is. Third, the blast radius consolidated. When production, the build system, the object storage, and the backups all live inside one cloud organisation reachable by one set of administrator credentials, a single identity compromise takes all four.

**Why this matters to you specifically.** This is the cell where the gap between what people believe and what is true is widest, and it is measurable in an afternoon. Almost every startup will tell you backups exist. Far fewer can name the last time a restore was actually performed, and fewer still can tell you how long a full restore takes or whether the restore path depends on the same credentials the attacker would already hold. You are not auditing whether backups exist. You are asking one question: has anyone ever gotten the data back, and how long did it take.

**Grid attachment.** Nothing in the 2019 grid covers this. It is cell M-6, and the procedure lives in `references/m-6-backups-and-recovery.md`. It also touches DR-1, because the incident response plan is fiction if the recovery step has never been executed, and CO-4, because you cannot decide what needs a backup until you know what data you hold.

**Concrete first action.** Pick the single data store whose permanent loss would end the company, and find the answers to four questions in writing: when did a restore last actually run, who ran it, how long did it take end to end, and can the credential that a compromised administrator would hold also delete the backups. Record the answers under M-6 in `SECURITY-STATE.md`. Do not perform a restore or a failover test yourself on the strength of this file: a restore test can consume real money, can overwrite live data if it is pointed at the wrong target, and can page an on-call engineer. It requires an explicit human yes, a named owner in engineering, and a target environment agreed in advance.

## What the 2019 grid got right, and why it holds up

Be genuinely fair here. The grid's durable value is not the sixteen cells, it is four structural insights that are still correct.

**Short scope beats deep scope.** Evan's framing of the whole playbook was that every item is short in scope, and that you can get roughly ninety-five percent of the way to initially addressing all sixteen in a single quarter. That is still the correct posture for a founding hire, and it is the antidote to the failure mode where a new hire disappears for two months building a threat model nobody reads. Breadth first, then depth where the breadth pass found blood.

**Your priorities depend on facts about the company, not on a framework.** Slide 16 listed what determines your priorities: business-to-business or business-to-consumer, company size, customer base, product, engineering velocity, and company culture, with the business model named as the single biggest determinant. That is exactly right and it is why this skill starts with recon and intake rather than a plan.

**Embedding with engineers beats gate-keeping them.** The SE-1 advice, that if there is no software development lifecycle structure you can create an inbound, ad-hoc review process, advertise it, deliver visible value, and let word spread, is more correct now than it was then. With a review-everything approach mathematically impossible at 2026 code volumes, reputation and the paved road are the only mechanisms that scale for one person.

**Corporate is tractable, production is not.** "Production takes years to fix and corp is something you can do in a few quarters" was the most underrated line in the talk. Everything in Themes 6 and 7 confirms it, and raises the stakes.

**Detection and response feels like no progress for a long time.** He said it plainly, and it is still the domain where a new hire is most likely to feel like a failure. Warn the human about this before they start DR work, not after.

**His four concrete starting signals aged well.** Cloud key usage, cloud configuration change monitoring, identity provider application access, and office name resolution traffic. Three of those four are still in the top handful in 2026. The fourth, office name resolution, is the one to swap out for endpoint telemetry when there is no office.

**And the questionnaire advice is unchanged.** Your highest and best use is not filling out security questionnaires for the sales team. Make it self-service or you will spend the year on it.

## The one cell people start too early

**SE-4, bug bounty.** Evan flagged it in 2019 with the parenthetical "hold off if you can" and said in the notes he had never had a truly great experience with one. That advice got stronger, not weaker.

Here is the argument to give a founder who wants one. A bug bounty is an inbound queue of unvetted reports, arriving at unpredictable times, that a solo security hire must triage personally, that generates a duplicate and low-quality report ratio typically well over half, and that creates an implicit response-time obligation to strangers. It converts your scarce hours into reactive work that is defined by outsiders. And it finds nothing you would not have found by running a free cloud posture scan and fixing your identity provider, because researchers find what is externally reachable and your worst problems right now are internally reachable. In 2026 there is an additional cost: automated and model-assisted submission volume has risen sharply, and the low-signal share of the queue with it.

What to do instead, and it takes an afternoon: publish a vulnerability disclosure policy and a `security.txt` file at the standard well-known path on your domain, with a monitored contact address and a plain statement that you will not pursue legal action against good-faith researchers. That captures the reports that would have reached you anyway, gives you a place to send the person who found something, satisfies most compliance questions about the topic, and costs nothing per report. See `references/se-4-bug-bounty-and-disclosure.md`.

Two runners-up worth naming. **Buying a compliance automation platform in month one**, covered in Theme 8. And **building a threat model of the product before you know what the product's data stores are**, which is SE-2 work disguised as SE-1 work.

## What to do with this file at runtime

- A theme becomes relevant when something you found in this company points at it, never because it is the next one in the list. State the theme in one sentence, name the grid cell, and propose exactly one next action, justified by the finding rather than by the theme's existence. Do not brief the human on all ten.
- When the human agrees to act, stop reading this file and load the procedure: `references/07-modern-cells.md` for M-1 through M-5, `references/m-6-backups-and-recovery.md` for M-6, and the individual cell file for anything on the 2019 grid.
- When a human asks why you are recommending something not on the slide, cite the theme number and the one-line reason. Do not relitigate the whole delta.
- Record every action taken under its grid cell in `SECURITY-STATE.md` with a status of unknown, none, partial, or done, plus the evidence.
- Record any decision to defer a theme in `DECISION-LOG.md` with the date, the reasoning, and who approved it. Deferring is a legitimate answer. Deferring silently is not.
- Any control that mutates production, locks a person out, costs money, or notifies a customer requires an explicit stated stop and a human yes first. Theme 6 enforcement and Theme 4 revocation both qualify.
